## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the BROADLINE RETAIL industry — Amazon, eBay, mass-market e-commerce and department stores — to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the platform-and-aggregation economics that defined Amazon's transformation of retail, why marketplace take-rates compound on scale, why the 2010s e-commerce shift permanently reshaped traditional department stores, and what the structurally healthy and structurally challenged corners of broadline retail look like today.

What to attend to particularly for Broadline Retail. The defining feature of modern broadline retail is the platform-vs-pipe distinction. (1) Amazon — the canonical platform-aggregator: third-party seller revenue (now over half of merchandise volume) earns marketplace take-rates of 10–20%; first-party retail is lower-margin but provides selection-leadership; AWS and advertising are the high-margin pieces that fund the retail flywheel. The single most informative piece of contemporary writing on Amazon's economics is Ben Thompson's Stratechery aggregation-theory framing. (2) Mass-market online retail — eBay (auction marketplace, structurally challenged through the 2010s as Amazon took share), Etsy (handmade and vintage, niche-marketplace economics), MercadoLibre (Latin America Amazon-equivalent), Coupang (Korea), JD.com and Pinduoduo (China). Each operates on similar economics — supply-side seller liquidity drives demand-side buyer attraction, network effects compound, and unit economics improve with scale. (3) Department stores — Macy's, Nordstrom, Kohl's, Dillard's. The structurally challenged piece; secular share loss to off-price (TJX, Ross), specialty (Lululemon, Sephora), and online retail. The 2017 Sears bankruptcy is the canonical extreme case. Most listed department stores have either consolidated, restructured around real-estate value (Macy's flagship store land), or shifted toward private-label brands and off-price formats. (4) Mass merchants and warehouse clubs — these technically sit in Consumer Staples Distribution & Retail (Walmart, Costco, Target, BJ's), so they're covered separately. The wisdom-tradition writings here include Buffett's Berkshire commentary on retail moats and Charlie Munger's Costco-via-the-board-of-directors commentary; Munger has been notably skeptical of Amazon as a long-only investment despite acknowledging its brilliance. The single most useful frame: distinguish the platform-with-take-rate economics (Amazon, MercadoLibre) from the inventory-on-balance-sheet retail economics (department stores, mass merchants); the former compound at structurally higher returns on capital because the marginal customer transaction has near-zero capital cost.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Stratechery by Ben Thompson — aggregation theory and the platform-vs-pipe distinction is the canonical contemporary primer for understanding Amazon-class businesses. The U.S. Census Bureau E-Commerce Retail Sales data. eMarketer / Insider Intelligence for online-retail share data where freely summarized. Damodaran's retail-sector data. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on network-effect moats. Specific industry trade press — RetailDive, Internet Retailer, Marketplace Pulse.

Length: 3,000–4,500 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Broadline Retail businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
