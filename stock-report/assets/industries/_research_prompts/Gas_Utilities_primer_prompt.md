## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the GAS UTILITIES industry — regulated natural-gas distribution and storage utilities — to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the rate-base / allowed-ROE economics that gas utilities share with electric utilities, the structural difference of the gas-distribution business model (lower capex per dollar of revenue than electric; less generation exposure; meaningful storage-and-transmission infrastructure), the secular pressure from electrification of buildings (heat pumps replacing gas furnaces), and the long-cycle tariff structures that govern revenue.

What to attend to particularly for Gas Utilities. The industry's defining feature is regulated natural-gas distribution under state public utility commission oversight, with rate-base / allowed-ROE economics structurally similar to electric utilities but with several distinguishing features. (1) Lower capex per dollar of revenue — gas distribution requires pipes and storage but no power-generation plants, so the capex / revenue ratio is typically 25–35% (vs 60–80% for vertically integrated electric utilities). (2) Storage matters — winter heating demand creates seasonal storage requirements that don't exist in electric; storage facilities (depleted gas reservoirs converted to storage) earn separate regulated returns. (3) Commodity-cost pass-through — natural-gas commodity costs are pass-through to customers in most jurisdictions, but the timing mismatch in extreme winter peaks (the 2021 Texas Uri freeze, the 2014 polar vortex) has produced significant disallowance risk. (4) Electrification pressure — California, Massachusetts, New York, and other states have begun policies that ban natural-gas hookups in new construction; the long-term residential demand profile is structurally challenged in cold-climate markets where building electrification is most policy-supported. The canonical names — Atmos Energy (Texas / Mid-South gas distribution; the largest pure-play U.S. gas LDC), Southwest Gas Holdings (Arizona/Nevada), Spire Inc. (Missouri / Alabama), New Jersey Resources, ONE Gas, National Fuel Gas (mixed gas-distribution and upstream gas), South Jersey Industries (acquired private 2023), UGI Corporation (mixed propane-and-gas-distribution), Black Hills Corporation (gas plus electric mixed). Multi-utilities (Sempra, Duke, NiSource, Dominion) have substantial gas-distribution exposure too. Wisdom-tradition references — Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway Energy includes both electric and gas distribution (Northern Natural Gas pipeline, Kern River); the broader rate-base / allowed-ROE framework applies. The single most useful frame: gas utilities share the regulated-monopoly economic engine with electric utilities but face structurally different secular trajectories — the electrification-of-buildings policy trajectory in cold-climate states is the dominant long-term question for residential gas distribution, while the industrial and power-generation gas-demand piece remains structurally healthy and growing.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway Energy commentary across Berkshire letters where gas distribution and pipelines appear. The American Gas Association (AGA) for industry-level data. NARUC for state-regulatory landscape. The EIA for natural-gas market data. Damodaran's gas-utilities data. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on regulated-monopoly moats. Specific industry trade press — Natural Gas Intelligence, NGI's Daily GPI.

Length: 3,000–4,500 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Gas Utilities businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
