## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the TEXTILES, APPAREL & LUXURY GOODS industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the structural difference between commodity textiles (Buffett's 1985 Burlington Industries cautionary tale), athletic apparel (Nike, Adidas), specialty branded apparel, and luxury goods (the LVMH-Kering-Hermès-Richemont ecosystem); why luxury is one of the highest-quality consumer-product business models and athletic apparel sometimes is too; and why most general apparel businesses revert to the mean.

What to attend to particularly for Textiles, Apparel & Luxury Goods. The industry's defining feature is brand-strength durability, and brand durability varies enormously across sub-segments. (1) Luxury goods — LVMH (Louis Vuitton, Dior, Tiffany, Bulgari, dozens more), Kering (Gucci, Saint Laurent, Bottega Veneta, Balenciaga), Hermès (the highest-quality of all by margin and pricing power), Richemont (Cartier, Van Cleef, IWC, Jaeger-LeCoultre, watches), Prada, Burberry. Hermès is the canonical case — operating margins consistently above 40%, pricing power that has compounded for decades, supply discipline (the Birkin bag waitlist is structural, not accidental), founder-family alignment (the Hermès family successfully fended off LVMH's 2010–14 hostile attempt). LVMH under Bernard Arnault is the canonical roll-up case in luxury. The economic moats are intangible-brand-driven, and in the strongest cases (Hermès, Louis Vuitton, Patek Philippe in watches) genuinely irreplicable. (2) Athletic apparel — Nike, Adidas, Lululemon, Under Armour, Puma, On Holding, Deckers (Hoka, Ugg). Nike is the canonical structurally healthy case across decades; Lululemon was the canonical recent compounder before the 2024 stumble; Under Armour and adidas have struggled through multiple cycles. Athlete sponsorship and product innovation cycle drive demand; long-cycle category trends (running, athleisure) compound faster than equity-cycle macro. (3) Specialty branded apparel — VF Corporation (The North Face, Vans, Timberland), Tapestry (Coach, Kate Spade, Stuart Weitzman), Capri Holdings (Michael Kors, Versace, Jimmy Choo), Levi Strauss, PVH (Tommy Hilfiger, Calvin Klein), Ralph Lauren, Carter's. Mid-tier brand portfolios; brand fatigue and category cyclicality are recurring risks. (4) Footwear — Skechers, Crocs, Birkenstock, Wolverine. Athletic-adjacent. (5) Manufacturers and contract apparel — Hanesbrands, Gildan Activewear. Lower-margin; closer to commodity. (6) Watches — Movado, Fossil. Generally challenged; Apple Watch has eaten the lower-luxury watch market. Wisdom-tradition reference: Buffett's 1985 letter on Berkshire's exit from the textile business is the canonical cautionary tale on commodity textiles ("Buffett's textile mill became the proverbial bad business; only a fool would have stayed in"). The broader Buffett commentary on consumer brands (Coca-Cola 1988, See's 1972/2007, the 2007 letter on the durability of consumer brand franchises) applies directly to luxury and athletic apparel.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Buffett's 1985 Berkshire textile-exit commentary; broader Buffett on consumer franchises across letters. Bain & Company's Luxury Goods Worldwide Market Study (annual; partial summaries are free). Damodaran's apparel and luxury data. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on brand moats. Specific industry trade press — Vogue Business, Business of Fashion, Sourcing Journal.

Length: 3,500–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Textiles, Apparel & Luxury Goods businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
