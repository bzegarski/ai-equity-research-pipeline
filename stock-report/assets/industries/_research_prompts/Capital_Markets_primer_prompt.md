## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the CAPITAL MARKETS industry — investment banks, broker-dealers, asset managers, exchanges, financial-data and ratings — to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the four very different sub-segments inside Capital Markets — investment banking and broker-dealers (cyclical fee businesses), asset managers (AUM-fee businesses with sticky retail and pension flows), financial exchanges (the highest-quality businesses in the entire Financials sector), and credit ratings agencies (oligopoly toll-booth economics) — and why the wisdom tradition treats exchanges and ratings agencies as among the highest-quality businesses in U.S. equity markets.

What to attend to particularly for Capital Markets. Four structurally different sub-segments. (1) Investment banks and broker-dealers — Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, Charles Schwab (the largest U.S. retail broker-dealer), Interactive Brokers, Raymond James, LPL Financial, Stifel Financial. Investment-banking revenue (M&A advisory, equity and debt underwriting) is deeply cyclical with the deal cycle; trading revenue is volatility-correlated and unpredictable; wealth management is the steadier piece that the major firms have built up over 20 years (Morgan Stanley's 2009 acquisition of Smith Barney, Goldman's organic wealth platform). Buffett's 2008 Goldman Sachs preferred-and-warrants investment is the canonical wisdom-tradition writeup of an investment bank in distress. (2) Asset managers — BlackRock (the largest, ~$10T AUM), Vanguard (private), State Street (also a custody bank), Invesco, T. Rowe Price, Franklin Resources, Affiliated Managers Group, Blackstone (alts), KKR (alts), Apollo Global (alts), Brookfield Asset Management. The traditional active-management piece has been under structural pressure from passive (ETF) growth for two decades; the alts piece (Blackstone, KKR, Apollo, Ares, Carlyle) has compounded materially faster as institutions and HNW investors have moved capital into alternatives. AUM-based fees are the structural advantage — sticky, recurring, and scalable at near-zero marginal cost. (3) Financial exchanges — CME Group (commodity and rates futures), Intercontinental Exchange (ICE; energy and softs futures, NYSE, mortgage-tech), Nasdaq, Cboe Global Markets, MarketAxess (electronic bond trading), Tradeweb (electronic bond trading), Coinbase (crypto trading; structurally different cycle exposure). Network-effect and coordination moats — once a futures contract trades on CME, the liquidity gravitates there and competing exchanges cannot easily build comparable depth. The wisdom tradition treats exchanges as among the highest-quality businesses in U.S. equity markets — operating margins of 50–60%, near-zero capital intensity, and long secular growth trajectories. (4) Credit ratings agencies — Moody's, S&P Global (S&P Ratings plus the broader information services), Fitch (private; a Hearst subsidiary). The S&P / Moody's duopoly captures most U.S. corporate-debt rating revenue and benefits from regulatory entrenchment (NRSRO designation, the issuer-pays business model that's hard to replace). Buffett's Moody's commentary is positive and load-bearing — Berkshire was a long-time large holder. (5) Trust and custody banks — Bank of New York Mellon, State Street, Northern Trust. Custody-and-administration fee businesses with significant securities-lending revenue. The wisdom-tradition references span Buffett's Goldman Sachs (2008 deal), American Express (technically Consumer Finance / capital markets adjacent), Moody's commentary; Howard Marks's Oaktree is itself a capital-markets business and his memos engage the dynamics of the industry directly.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Buffett's Goldman Sachs (2008 deal letter) and Moody's commentary across Berkshire letters. Howard Marks's Oaktree memos (the entire body engages capital-markets cycle behavior). The SEC's Form ADV filings for asset-manager AUM disclosures. ICI (Investment Company Institute) for U.S. fund-industry data. The World Federation of Exchanges (WFE) for global-exchange volume data. Damodaran's data on capital-markets sectors. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on network-effect and toll-booth moats with worked exchange-and-ratings examples.

Length: 4,000–5,500 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Capital Markets businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
