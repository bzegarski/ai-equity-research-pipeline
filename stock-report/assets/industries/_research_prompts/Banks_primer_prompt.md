## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the BANKS industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the net interest margin (NIM) economics that define banking, the credit cycle's role in cumulative through-the-cycle return on equity, the regulatory capital framework (CET1, Tier 1 leverage), why deposits are the most valuable asset and bank-specific deposit franchises differ enormously, and the Buffett wisdom-tradition writings on banking that span six decades.

What to attend to particularly for Banks. Banks are the wisdom tradition's deepest territory after Insurance. Buffett has written about Wells Fargo (1990 letter — the canonical purchase commentary; held 1990–2020, exited at material loss after the fake-accounts scandal), American Express (1964 Salad Oil discovery purchase, 1991 letter on the franchise, held through 2025), Bank of America (2011 preferred-and-warrants deal during the post-crisis stress; the canonical Buffett bank investment of the modern era), M&T Bank, U.S. Bancorp (held until trimmed), and decades of broader commentary. Howard Marks's 2007–2009 cycle memos are the canonical writing on bank-cycle behavior. The economics: a bank earns the spread between asset yields (loans, securities) and funding costs (deposits, debt), times leverage. Net interest margin (NIM = (interest income − interest expense) / interest-earning assets) is the central metric. Three structural questions matter for any bank. (1) Deposit franchise — low-cost, sticky deposits (consumer checking accounts, small-business operating accounts, sweep accounts) are the structural advantage; brokered deposits and CDs are not. The 2023 Silicon Valley Bank failure exposed how concentrated, hot-money deposits could collapse a bank in a week. (2) Credit cycle — bank earnings look stable in expansion years (low charge-offs, low provisions) and collapse in recessions (rising NPLs, large provisions, dilutive capital raises). The cumulative through-the-cycle return on equity is what matters; peak-cycle ROE is misleading. The 1990 commercial real estate cycle, the 2008 mortgage cycle, the 2020 pandemic provision spike, and the 2023 commercial-real-estate office stress are the canonical recent stress tests. (3) Regulatory capital — CET1 ratio (Common Equity Tier 1 / risk-weighted assets) plus Tier 1 leverage are the binding capital constraints under Basel III; the strongest banks earn returns above their regulatory capital cost while maintaining excess capital buffer. The canonical names span scale. Money-center banks — JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, Citigroup. Regional and super-regional banks — U.S. Bancorp, Truist Financial (BB&T-SunTrust merger 2019), PNC Financial, Capital One (also Consumer Finance), M&T Bank, Fifth Third Bancorp, KeyCorp, Huntington Bancshares, Regions Financial, Citizens Financial. Trust banks and asset-management banks — Bank of New York Mellon, State Street, Northern Trust. Online-and-tech-driven — Charles Schwab (also Capital Markets), JPMorgan's online piece, Wells Fargo's online piece, plus pure digital banks (SoFi). Foreign-listed comparables — HSBC, Royal Bank of Canada, TD, BMO, Itaú, Santander.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Buffett's bank letters across Berkshire (1990 Wells Fargo, 1991 AmEx, 2011 BofA preferred deal, multi-decade commentary; cite each year). Howard Marks's 2007–2009 cycle memos — "Risk" (January 2006), "It's All Good" (July 2007), "The Limits to Negativism" (October 2008), "The Long View" (January 2009). The Federal Reserve's FEDS Notes for plain-English research on banking mechanics. The FDIC Quarterly Banking Profile. The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) for Basel framework primers. Damodaran's bank-sector data. Pat Dorsey's Little Book chapter on bank moats and deposit franchises.

Length: 4,000–6,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Banks in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
