## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the PASSENGER AIRLINES industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for why Buffett has historically called airlines a "death trap" for investors, what the four structural problems are (commodity-priced output, very high fixed costs, capacity additions that arrive at the wrong time, labor-cost inflation), why Berkshire Hathaway nonetheless held a basket of airline positions from 2016 through 2020 and what changed his mind, and what (if anything) about the industry's economics has structurally improved.

What to attend to particularly for Passenger Airlines. The wisdom-tradition reference for this industry is Warren Buffett, with two distinct chapters. (1) The 1989–2007 chapters: Buffett's USAir disaster (1989 preferred-stock investment that lost most of its value) produced a generation of "I will never own an airline again" commentary in the annual letters. The 2007 letter is canonical: airlines are commodity-priced output, high fixed costs, periodic capacity oversupply, and a labor relations cycle that captures all surplus. (2) The 2016–2020 chapter: Berkshire bought positions in Delta, American, United, and Southwest in 2016, citing post-bankruptcy industry consolidation (the 2008–2013 wave of mergers and Chapter 11 filings reduced the major U.S. carriers from 9 to 4) and observed pricing discipline. The 2020 pandemic exit at substantial losses demonstrated that even consolidated, the industry's exposure to demand collapse remains catastrophic — Buffett's 2020 letter on the airline exit is the canonical recent commentary. The four structural problems remain: commodity-priced output (the customer's primary purchase driver is price, not airline preference, on most routes); very high fixed costs (aircraft leases, debt service, gate slots, labor contracts); capacity additions that arrive at the wrong time (a 787 ordered in 2018 arrives in 2024; the airline cannot delay delivery without contractual penalty); and labor-cost cycles where unionized pilot, flight attendant, and mechanic groups capture surplus during good years. The canonical names are Delta, American, United, Southwest, Alaska, JetBlue, Spirit (in bankruptcy), Frontier, Allegiant, Hawaiian (acquired by Alaska 2024). The Ryanair / easyJet European low-cost model is structurally healthier than the U.S. legacy-carrier model. International long-haul is more concentrated and earns better returns than domestic. The frequent-flyer program is a financial asset of meaningful scale (Delta's SkyMiles is estimated at $20–$30B in standalone value); it is the most reliable cash-generating piece of most legacy carriers and worth understanding separately.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Buffett's commentary on airlines across Berkshire letters (1989 USAir era through 2007 cautionary letter, 2016 buy-thesis commentary, 2020 exit commentary). The U.S. Department of Transportation T-100 segment data (free) for traffic and capacity statistics. Airlines for America (A4A) for industry-level data. The Airline Reporting Corporation. Damodaran's airline-industry data. Specific industry trade press — Cranky Flier, the Jon Ostrower-edited Airline Weekly, Skift.

Length: 3,500–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Passenger Airlines businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
