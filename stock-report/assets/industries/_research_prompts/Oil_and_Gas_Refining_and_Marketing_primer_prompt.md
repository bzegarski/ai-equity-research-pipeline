## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the OIL & GAS REFINING & MARKETING industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for how a refining and marketing business actually works — the crack-spread economics, the role of refinery complexity, the geographic dynamics of crude differentials, the counter-cyclical relationship to upstream margins, and the historical analogs that recur.

What to attend to particularly for Oil & Gas Refining & Marketing. Refiners earn the spread between crude inputs and refined product outputs — gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, asphalt, petrochemicals. That spread (the "crack spread"; the 3-2-1 crack is the canonical proxy: 3 barrels of crude in, 2 barrels of gasoline plus 1 barrel of distillate out) is the single most important number for the industry, and it is brutally cyclical. Capacity utilization runs 85–95% in normal periods; below 85% is brutal because fixed costs dominate. The Nelson Complexity Index measures a refinery's ability to handle heavier, sourer (higher-sulfur) crudes — more complex refineries earn higher margins because they buy cheaper feedstock. Geographic positioning around U.S. PADD regions matters because crude price differentials (WTI vs Brent vs Western Canadian Select vs Mars) move independently. The canonical names are Valero Energy, Marathon Petroleum, Phillips 66, HF Sinclair, PBF Energy. Refining is structurally counter-cyclical to upstream — when crude collapses, refining margins often expand because pump prices reset more slowly than wholesale crude. The 2008–09 collapse, the 2020 pandemic distillate-margin spike, and the 2022 post-Russia diesel margin boom each teach a different lesson. The wisdom-tradition writers have written less about refining than about most industries, but Howard Marks's commodity-cycle memos apply directly. Capex is meaningful but lumpy — turnarounds (planned maintenance shutdowns) every 4–6 years per unit. Marketing (gas stations, convenience stores) is a very different business — more retail than refining — and most pure refiners have spun out or sold their retail networks.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. The EIA's weekly petroleum status report (free; the canonical source for refinery utilization, gasoline and distillate inventories, crack spreads). The American Fuel & Petrochemical Manufacturers (AFPM) industry data. RBN Energy's Daily Blog for refining-specific commentary. Howard Marks on commodity cycles. Damodaran's energy data. Wood Mackenzie summaries where freely available. Specific industry trade press — Argus Media, Platts.

Length: 3,000–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Oil & Gas Refining & Marketing businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
