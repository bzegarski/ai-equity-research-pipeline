## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the OIL & GAS EXPLORATION & PRODUCTION (E&P) industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for how a pure-play upstream business actually works — the dominant economic engine, the metrics that move the value, the moat shapes that are possible (mostly: low-cost basin position is the only durable one), the typical risk pattern across commodity cycles, and the historical analogs that recur.

What to attend to particularly for Oil & Gas Exploration & Production. The reader needs to understand four concepts before any company-specific facts can be interpreted. (1) Reserve replacement — annual additions to proved reserves vs annual production; below 100% means the company is shrinking its asset base. (2) Finding-and-development cost per barrel — the all-in capital cost of replacing a barrel of production, the single number that defines whether a basin is profitable at a given oil price. (3) Decline curves — shale oil declines 60–80% in year 1 and 30–40% in year 2 before flattening; conventional fields decline 4–6% annually. The math of "how many wells do I have to drill just to keep production flat" is brutal in shale and gentle in conventional. (4) Hedging policy — some companies hedge 50–80% of forward 12-month production, smoothing realized prices; others don't, leaving full upside and full downside. The canonical names are Pioneer Natural Resources, Devon, EOG Resources, Hess, Diamondback, Continental Resources, Coterra. Howard Marks's commodity cycle thinking applies directly — the 1986 oil collapse, the 1998 Asian-crisis low, the 2014–16 shale glut, the 2020 pandemic plus negative WTI day, and the 2022 Russia/Ukraine spike each teach a different lesson about cycle psychology. Munger and Buffett's general skepticism of commodity-business moats is load-bearing here; in pure E&P, the moat (where it exists) is low-cost basin position — Permian Basin acreage, Bakken core, Eagle Ford sweet spots. The one-sentence frame: this is a price-taker on a depleting asset; the long-run winners are the lowest-cost producers in the best basins with the most disciplined capital allocators.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Howard Marks on commodity cycles. The EIA's Today in Energy and Drilling Productivity Report (free monthly). The Society of Petroleum Engineers (SPE) reserves-reporting standards. Damodaran's energy data. Goehring & Rozencwajg market commentary. Specific industry references — RBN Energy, Wood Mackenzie summaries. Buffett's commentary on commodity-business moats (especially the 2007 letter and his Occidental commentary).

Length: 3,000–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Oil & Gas E&P businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
