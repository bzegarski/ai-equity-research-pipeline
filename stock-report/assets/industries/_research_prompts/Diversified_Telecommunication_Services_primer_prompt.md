## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the DIVERSIFIED TELECOMMUNICATION SERVICES industry — wireline carriers, integrated wireline-and-wireless, cable-and-broadband — to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the capex-heavy infrastructure economics that define wireline and broadband, the structural shift from declining wireline voice to broadband internet as the durable revenue driver, the cord-cutting trajectory that eroded cable's TV revenue while broadband held up, and the secular fiber-build-out and rural-broadband subsidies reshaping the industry through 2025–2030.

What to attend to particularly for Diversified Telecommunication Services. The industry's defining feature is capital-intensive infrastructure with regulated returns on portions of the asset base. (1) Integrated incumbents — AT&T, Verizon Communications. Wireline + wireless + (formerly) media; both have spent the last decade divesting non-core assets (AT&T spun WarnerMedia 2022; Verizon spun Yahoo / AOL). The wireline business is in long secular decline (consumer voice down ~50% over 15 years; small-and-medium-business voice declining); the wireless business (covered separately under Wireless Telecommunication Services) is the durable piece. Buffett's brief 2020 Verizon position (held only ~6 quarters before exit) is a recent wisdom-tradition engagement and the resulting commentary is informative. (2) Cable and broadband — Comcast, Charter Communications, Altice USA, Cable One, WideOpenWest. Cable companies have reinvented themselves as broadband-and-streaming-and-mobile bundles since 2015; the structural growth has come from broadband (steady ARPU growth, share gain from DSL) while traditional cable-TV bundles lost subscribers to streaming. Comcast's NBCUniversal (formerly classified Communication Services-Media) layered media-and-theme-parks on top. The 2024–25 fixed-wireless-access threat from T-Mobile and Verizon home internet is the structural challenge to cable broadband moats. (3) Fiber-focused — Lumen Technologies (formerly CenturyLink; secular decline plus enterprise piece), Frontier Communications (post-bankruptcy fiber pure-play), Consolidated Communications. Fiber-build-out economics (per-mile capex, take-rate at the home, payback periods of 7–12 years) are the central operating question. (4) Rural and specialty — Iridium Communications (satellite voice and data; structurally different), TDS / United States Cellular (rural wireless plus cable), Cogent Communications (B2B fiber backhaul). The wisdom-tradition references include Buffett's 2020 Verizon position commentary; Howard Marks's writing on capital-heavy slow-secular-decline businesses applies; Pat Dorsey's Little Book on regulatory-and-physical-asset moats applies but with the cautionary frame that telecom's moats have eroded steadily as wireless substitution and fiber-overbuild have reduced the structural advantages of incumbent wireline networks.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Buffett's brief 2020 Verizon commentary across Berkshire letters. The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) annual reports for U.S. telecom market data. The NCTA (the Internet & Television Association) for cable industry data. USTelecom for industry-level commentary. Damodaran's telecom-sector data. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on infrastructure moats. Specific industry trade press — Light Reading, FierceTelecom, Communications Daily.

Length: 3,500–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Diversified Telecommunication Services businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
