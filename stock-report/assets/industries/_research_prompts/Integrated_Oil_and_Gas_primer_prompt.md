## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the INTEGRATED OIL & GAS industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for how an Integrated Oil & Gas business actually works — the dominant economic engine, the metrics that move the value, the moat shapes that are possible (and the ones that are not), the typical risk pattern across commodity cycles, and the historical analogs that recur. When I then read a writeup of a single integrated, the primer should arm me to ask the right questions.

What to attend to particularly for Integrated Oil & Gas. The defining feature is vertical integration — the same company owns the wellhead, the pipeline, the refinery, and (often) the gas station — and the argument for it is that profit pools shift across the value chain through the commodity cycle, so the integrated portfolio looks smoother across decades than any pure-play piece. ExxonMobil, Chevron, Shell, BP, and TotalEnergies are the canonical names. Through an oil-price collapse, upstream cash flow falls and downstream margins (refining, marketing) often expand because input costs drop while pump prices reset more slowly. The central management question, repeated annually, is capital allocation through the cycle — buybacks at the bottom or the top, dividend defense vs growth investment, the willingness to walk away from low-return projects rather than chase production growth. Buffett's PetroChina position (2002 purchase, 2007 letter explaining the exit) and the Berkshire Occidental and Chevron positions are the wisdom-tradition writings closest to dispositive on integrateds — pull from them in detail. Reserves replacement, finding-and-development costs, and capex/operating-cash-flow ratio are the metrics that matter; ROCE through a full cycle is the truer test than peak-year ROIC. The energy-transition narrative and the management response (continued upstream investment vs renewables pivot vs distribution to shareholders) is the dominant present strategic question.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Buffett's PetroChina commentary (2007 letter especially) and his Occidental / Chevron commentary across recent annual letters. Howard Marks on commodity cycles. The EIA's Today in Energy series and the BP Statistical Review of World Energy as primer-level industry references. Damodaran's energy-sector data (pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar). Daniel Yergin's The Prize and The Quest for the historical sweep. Specific industry trade press — Oil and Gas Journal, Wood Mackenzie summaries where freely available. Goehring & Rozencwajg quarterly market commentary for thoughtful contemporary writing.

Length: 3,000–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Integrated Oil & Gas businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
