## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the CONSUMER FINANCE industry — credit cards, auto lenders, payday and consumer-installment lenders — to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the credit-loss cycle that dominates consumer-finance economics, the difference between premium-card franchises (American Express, Discover) and subprime-and-near-prime lenders (Capital One, Synchrony, Credit Acceptance), the structural advantages of Buffett's longest-held position (American Express, 1964–present), and the regulatory and consumer-protection environment shaping the industry.

What to attend to particularly for Consumer Finance. The industry's defining feature is credit-cycle exposure on a consumer-credit book. (1) Premium credit-card franchises — American Express, Discover Financial Services. AmEx is the canonical wisdom-tradition reference: Buffett's 1964 purchase during the Salad Oil Scandal (Warren bought ~5% of AmEx during a brief share-price collapse over a fraud at one subsidiary), the 1991 letter explaining why AmEx's franchise was undervalued and the position was retained, and the multi-decade compounding through 2025 as one of the longest-held Berkshire positions. The structural advantages — closed-loop network (AmEx is both issuer and merchant acquirer for its cards), affluent cardholder base with low loss rates, premium spending share — produce a meaningfully different risk profile than mass-market credit-card issuers. (2) Mass-market credit-card issuers — Capital One Financial (also classified Banks), Synchrony Financial, Bread Financial (formerly Alliance Data), Discover (technically here too). Subprime and near-prime credit exposure; cyclical loss rates; structurally lower credit quality than premium franchises. The 2007–09 cycle saw mass-market issuers absorb 7–10% peak loss rates; the 2020 pandemic cycle saw a brief spike followed by stimulus-driven loss reduction; the 2024–25 cycle has seen elevated charge-offs from the post-pandemic credit-quality reset. (3) Auto lenders — Ally Financial, Credit Acceptance Corporation (subprime auto specialist; structural cautionary case study with an AlphaArchitect-published deep dive showing the multi-decade compounding alongside the pre-2024 stock collapse). (4) Consumer-installment and payday — World Acceptance, Regional Management, Curo Group, OneMain Financial. The most cyclically exposed corner; CFPB regulatory scrutiny is a chronic headwind. (5) Buy-now-pay-later (BNPL) — Affirm Holdings, Klarna (private), Afterpay (acquired by Block 2021). The newest sub-segment; structurally different unit economics from credit cards (typically merchant-pay rather than consumer-pay); credit-loss curves are still maturing. The wisdom-tradition references center on AmEx — Buffett's commentary across letters, the 1964 Salad Oil reference (which is itself one of Graham's "margin of safety" worked examples in updated editions of Security Analysis), Munger's commentary on AmEx's franchise. Howard Marks on consumer-credit cycles applies broadly.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Buffett's American Express commentary across Berkshire letters (1991 onward; cite each year). The Federal Reserve's Consumer Credit (G.19) data (free monthly). The Federal Reserve's Senior Loan Officer Survey for consumer-credit underwriting standards. The CFPB Consumer Credit Trends. Howard Marks on credit cycles. Damodaran's consumer-finance data. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on consumer-finance moats with explicit AmEx treatment.

Length: 3,500–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Consumer Finance businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
