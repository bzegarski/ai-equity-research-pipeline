## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the PHARMACEUTICALS industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the patent-cliff economics that define pharma — long fixed development costs, winner-take-most rewards inside a category, then a patent expiration that takes 80–90% of revenue away in 12 months — and the central analytical question of "what's in the pipeline against what comes off patent." I should also have a feel for the regulatory / pricing environment that shapes the U.S. industry's profitability.

What to attend to particularly for Pharmaceuticals. The industry's defining feature is patent-cycle economics. A successful drug enjoys monopoly pricing for the patent-protected period (typically 12–14 years of effective marketing exclusivity after FDA approval), then loses 80–90% of revenue within 12 months when generic competition enters. The central analytical question for any large-cap pharma is the pipeline-vs-LOE (loss of exclusivity) balance: what is the upcoming wave of patent expirations, and what is in clinical development to replace the lost revenue. The 2024–28 patent-cliff wave for the industry includes Keytruda (Merck; ~$25B annual revenue), Eliquis (Bristol-Myers Squibb / Pfizer), Opdivo (BMS), Stelara (Johnson & Johnson; already losing exclusivity), Imbruvica (AbbVie / Johnson & Johnson). The canonical names span large-cap and specialty. (1) Large-cap branded pharma — Pfizer, Merck, Eli Lilly (the canonical recent compounder, with GLP-1 franchise of Mounjaro and Zepbound transforming the company through 2024–25), Johnson & Johnson, Bristol-Myers Squibb, AbbVie (post-Allergan acquisition), Amgen, Roche (Switzerland-listed), Novartis (Switzerland-listed), AstraZeneca (UK-listed), Sanofi (France-listed), GSK, Novo Nordisk (Denmark-listed; the Lilly counterpart in GLP-1 with Ozempic and Wegovy). (2) Specialty pharma — Royalty Pharma (royalty-stream investor in approved drugs), Jazz Pharmaceuticals, Catalent, ICU Medical. (3) Generics and biosimilars — Teva, Viatris (formed from Mylan / Pfizer Upjohn 2020), Sandoz (Novartis spin 2023), Hikma, Dr Reddy's. Structurally lower-margin than branded; subject to chronic pricing pressure. (4) Animal health — Zoetis, Elanco, IDEXX Laboratories (also Health Care Equipment). Structurally healthier than human pharma in some ways — less price regulation, more durable brand premiums. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 (Medicare Part D drug-price negotiation, starting 2026) and the broader U.S. drug-pricing pressure are the dominant present regulatory question. The wisdom-tradition position has been characteristic skepticism of pharma's complexity — Buffett briefly held J&J and made small Sanofi / Pfizer / Merck purchases in 2020–21 (then trimmed); his 2007 Berkshire letter and broader commentary have been candid about pharma being outside his most-comfortable circle of competence. Munger has commented favorably on pharma's defensive characteristics at Daily Journal annual meetings.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. The FDA Orange Book (free; canonical patent-and-exclusivity database). Clinicaltrials.gov for trial-stage and primary-endpoint context. PhRMA fact sheets on industry economics. CMS for Medicare Part D and the IRA drug-pricing negotiation list. Buffett's J&J / Pfizer / Merck / Sanofi commentary across recent Berkshire letters (2020–21). Damodaran's pharmaceutical-sector data (pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar). Specific industry trade press — Endpoints News, STAT News, FiercePharma.

Length: 3,500–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Pharmaceuticals businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
