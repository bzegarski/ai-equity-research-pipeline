## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the SOFTWARE industry — application software, infrastructure software, hyperscale cloud, vertical SaaS — to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the SaaS metrics (ARR, NRR, magic number, Rule of 40, RPO, customer-acquisition payback) that are the canonical first-language of public software investing, the very different economics of pure software vs hyperscale cloud (which is increasingly capital-intensive in the AI era), the moat shapes that durable software businesses earn (network effects, switching costs, data-asset moats), and the contemporary practitioner literature (Stratechery, Mauboussin, Bessemer) that frames the industry's economic logic.

What to attend to particularly for Software. The industry has three structurally different sub-segments that a primer needs to acknowledge separately. (1) Application software (SaaS) — Salesforce, Adobe, Intuit, Microsoft (the consumer-and-enterprise productivity piece), ServiceNow, Workday, HubSpot, Atlassian, Datadog, Snowflake, MongoDB, Veeva (also classified Health Care Tech), Shopify, Block. The defining metrics are ARR (Annualized Recurring Revenue), NRR (Net Revenue Retention; >120% is best-in-class for enterprise SaaS), RPO (Remaining Performance Obligations; the contracted backlog), Rule of 40 (revenue growth + FCF margin should sum to 40+; 60+ is excellent), magic number (net new ARR / prior-quarter S&M, annualized; >0.75 is healthy), customer-acquisition payback (months to recover CAC; 12–24 months for enterprise SaaS). Each metric should be defined inline using a real-company number. (2) Hyperscale cloud (IaaS, PaaS) — Microsoft Azure, Amazon AWS, Google Cloud Platform, Oracle Cloud Infrastructure. The capital-intensity story has changed structurally with the AI capex cycle. Microsoft's calendar-2026 capex of approximately $190B is industrial-business intensity (~60% of expected revenue), not software-business intensity (the historical 3–7%). The hyperscalers are increasingly closer to capital-heavy infrastructure businesses than pure software businesses, and reading them as such requires a different mental model than reading Salesforce or Adobe. The finance-lease-vs-operating-lease distinction matters because finance-lease principal doesn't go through capex, so reported FCF understates true infrastructure cost. (3) Infrastructure software — Oracle, SAP, IBM (also IT Services), Palo Alto Networks (security), CrowdStrike, Zscaler, Cloudflare, Fortinet, Splunk (acquired by Cisco), Elastic, GitLab. The wisdom-tradition writings on Software are sparse from the canonical books (Buffett's Apple position is consumer-brand, not software per se; the IBM cautionary tale is the closest direct engagement). The contemporary practitioner literature is essential — Stratechery by Ben Thompson on aggregation theory and platform-vs-pipe dynamics; Bessemer's State of the Cloud report (annual; canonical SaaS-economics primer); SaaStr for SaaS-metrics primers; Mauboussin's writings on intangible assets, growth durability, and the math of value-and-growth in software; a16z's "16 startup metrics" essays; Tomasz Tunguz, John Luttig (Founders Fund), Patrick OShaughnessy (Invest Like the Best). The single most useful frame: distinguish pure-software businesses (where capex is 3–7% of revenue and free cash flow approximates GAAP earnings adjusted for SBC) from hyperscale cloud businesses (where capex is 30–60%+ of revenue during the AI build-out and reading reported FCF requires understanding finance-lease accounting).

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Stratechery by Ben Thompson — aggregation theory and platform-vs-pipe is the canonical contemporary primer. Bessemer Venture Partners' State of the Cloud reports. SaaStr for SaaS-metrics primers. a16z's "16 startup metrics" and "16 more startup metrics" essays. Mauboussin's Counterpoint Global / Morgan Stanley papers on intangible-investment-driven growth. Damodaran's tech-sector data and Narrative and Numbers. Howard Marks's "bubble.com" memo (January 2000) for the canonical writeup of internet-era bubble psychology. The IDC and Gartner research summaries where freely available.

Length: 4,000–5,500 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Software businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
