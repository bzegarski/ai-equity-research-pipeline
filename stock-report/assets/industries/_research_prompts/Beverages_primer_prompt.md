## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the BEVERAGES industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the concentrate-and-syrup business model that defines Coca-Cola's economics, the very different economics of premium spirits and beer, why beverages is the deepest territory of the wisdom tradition outside Insurance, and how to recognize the brand-and-distribution moat that produces decades of pricing power against essentially flat unit volume.

What to attend to particularly for Beverages. The industry has three structurally different sub-segments. (1) Non-alcoholic beverages — Coca-Cola, PepsiCo, Keurig Dr Pepper, Monster Beverage, Celsius. The Coca-Cola business model is the canonical wisdom-tradition reference and the deepest single body of writing on a beverage company in equity literature. The 1988 Buffett purchase letter, the 1989 follow-up, the 1993 letter (calling Coca-Cola "inevitable"), and the multi-decade compounding through 2025 are essential reading. The structural insight: Coca-Cola sells concentrate to bottlers who handle the capital-intensive bottling-and-distribution piece; the parent company keeps the brand and pricing power and earns 30%+ operating margins on near-zero capital intensity. PepsiCo runs an integrated model (food + drink + bottling) at lower returns on capital but better diversification. Energy drinks (Monster, Celsius, Red Bull which is private) are a structurally faster-growth sub-category with similar concentrated-demand-and-strong-brand economics. (2) Alcoholic beverages — split between premium spirits (Diageo, Pernod Ricard, Constellation Brands, Brown-Forman) and beer (Anheuser-Busch InBev, Heineken, Molson Coors, Constellation's beer division). Premium spirits is the most attractive piece structurally — long aging cycles create supply discipline (a 12-year-old Scotch must be aged 12 years; you cannot ramp), brand and category-position drive pricing power, and demand-side trends (premiumization, cocktail-culture, the bourbon boom 2010–2024) have produced multi-decade compounding for the strongest operators. Beer is more challenged structurally — younger consumers drink less beer; brand premium has compressed; craft beer emergence eroded big-brand share through 2010s. (3) Wine and craft spirits — smaller listed universe; Constellation Brands' wine portfolio, Bottega Veneta's wine investments, plus regional listed names. Wisdom-tradition writings beyond Coca-Cola: Buffett on his early Bordeaux discoveries (commentary across letters); Munger on Costco's beverage attach; the broader brand-moat treatment in the 2007 Berkshire letter on durable consumer franchises; Pat Dorsey on the difficulty of brand moats in commodity-flavored categories vs the structural strength of premium-spirits categories.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Buffett's Coca-Cola commentary across Berkshire letters (1988, 1989, 1993, 1996, 2007, and onward — cite each year). The IWSR Drinks Market Analysis for spirits and wine industry data. The Beer Institute and Brewers Association for beer-industry data. NielsenIQ / Circana scanner data summaries where freely available. Damodaran's beverages data. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on brand moats in beverages.

Length: 3,500–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Beverages businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
