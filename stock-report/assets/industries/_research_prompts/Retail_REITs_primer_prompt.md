## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the RETAIL REITs industry — shopping center REITs, mall REITs, net-lease retail REITs — to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the structurally distinct economics of net-lease retail (Realty Income's monthly-dividend "The Monthly Dividend Company" model with triple-net 10–25 year leases to creditworthy tenants), grocery-anchored shopping centers (the resilient piece of multi-tenant retail), and Class A regional mall REITs (the most challenged piece). I should understand which tenant types create durable rent vs which create rent at risk in the e-commerce era.

What to attend to particularly for Retail REITs. The industry has three structurally distinct sub-segments with very different cycle and secular exposure. (1) Net-lease single-tenant retail — Realty Income (the canonical case; "The Monthly Dividend Company"; ~15,500 properties leased to 1,500+ tenants on triple-net 10–25 year leases; consistent monthly distributions for 50+ years), Agree Realty, NETSTREIT, Spirit Realty (acquired by Realty Income 2024), W. P. Carey (formerly Diversified; net-lease industrial focus post-2024 simplification), STORE Capital (acquired private 2022), Essential Properties Realty Trust. The structural advantage of net-lease retail is contractual — long leases with built-in rent escalators (typically 1–2% annually or CPI-linked), tenant responsible for taxes, insurance, and maintenance (the "triple net"), and creditworthy tenant base (Walgreens, Dollar General, Family Dollar, FedEx, 7-Eleven, Walmart). Realty Income is the canonical compounder with multi-decade dividend growth and the most-studied real-estate operating model in U.S. equity markets. (2) Grocery-anchored and necessity shopping centers — Kimco Realty, Federal Realty Investment Trust (the canonical premium operator with 50+ years of dividend increases), Regency Centers, Brixmor Property Group, Phillips Edison & Company (private until 2021 IPO), SITE Centers (formerly Developers Diversified), Acadia Realty Trust. The key tenant question is the grocery anchor — Kroger, Albertsons, Publix, Whole Foods, Trader Joe's-anchored centers have proven structurally resilient because the grocery-anchor traffic supports satellite tenants. The specialty-tenant mix in the satellite spaces is where rent at risk concentrates (the 2010s closing waves of Bed Bath & Beyond, Pier 1, Christmas Tree Shops, Rite Aid stores, and other middle-tier retailers). (3) Mall and trophy-retail REITs — Simon Property Group (the canonical Class A mall operator; the largest by far, with multi-decade compounding through 2019 followed by structural reset), Macerich, Tanger Outlet Centers (outlet-mall focus), Brookfield Property REIT (private). Mall REITs have been the most challenged corner of retail real estate over the past decade — anchor department stores (Sears, JC Penney, Macy's) have downsized or bankrupted, and the structural decline of mid-tier mall traffic has hurt occupancy and re-leasing rates. The Class A mall thesis (Simon's flagship trophy malls in major metros) remains structurally healthier than Class B and Class C malls, which have mostly become candidates for redevelopment, mixed-use conversion, or demolition. The wisdom-tradition references for this industry are sparse beyond NAREIT primer material — Realty Income's investor-relations material is the canonical practitioner-style writeup of net-lease economics; Federal Realty's investor presentations are similarly canonical for grocery-anchored. The single most useful frame: separate the structurally healthy net-lease (Realty Income) and grocery-anchored (Federal Realty, Kimco) sub-segments from the structurally challenged mall sub-segment.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. NAREIT REIT 101 series. Realty Income and Federal Realty investor-relations material as canonical primer references for net-lease and grocery-anchored economics. The International Council of Shopping Centers (ICSC) for industry-level data. CBRE and JLL retail-market reports. Damodaran's retail-REIT data. Specific industry trade press — Chain Store Age, Retail Dive, GlobeSt.

Length: 3,500–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Retail REITs in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
