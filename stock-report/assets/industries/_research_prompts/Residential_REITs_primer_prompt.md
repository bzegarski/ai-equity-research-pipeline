## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the RESIDENTIAL REITs industry — apartment REITs, single-family rental REITs, manufactured housing REITs — to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the structurally distinct economics of apartment REITs (one-year leases, frequent re-pricing, demand correlated with employment) vs single-family rental REITs (longer holding periods, repair-and-maintenance expense burden, scattered geographic operations) vs manufactured-housing REITs (the highest-quality structural piece, with the underlying land producing utility-like cash flows beneath the homes). I should also understand the regional supply-and-demand dynamics that drive Sun Belt vs coastal apartment markets in opposite directions over decade-long cycles.

What to attend to particularly for Residential REITs. The industry has three structurally distinct sub-segments. (1) Apartment REITs — Equity Residential (Sam Zell's old company; coastal-and-urban focus), AvalonBay Communities (coastal-and-urban focus), Camden Property Trust (Sun Belt focus), Mid-America Apartment Communities (Sun Belt focus), Essex Property Trust (West Coast / California), UDR, Independence Realty Trust, Apartment Income REIT (post-2020 AIMCO simplification), Apartment Investment and Management Company (AIMCO; the legacy older complex now simplified), NexPoint Residential Trust. The cyclical dynamics — apartment leases are typically one year; rent can re-price quickly to market; the 2021–22 rent spike (median U.S. apartment rents up 15–25%) and the 2023–24 partial reset is the canonical recent cycle. The Sun Belt vs coastal divide is the central geographic question — Sun Belt markets (Atlanta, Phoenix, Tampa, Austin, Charlotte, Nashville) have benefited from population migration and lower regulatory burden; coastal markets (San Francisco, NYC, LA, DC) have faced structural population outflows and rent-control regulatory pressure. (2) Single-family rental (SFR) REITs — Invitation Homes (the largest), American Homes 4 Rent, Tricon Residential (acquired by Blackstone 2024). SFR economics differ materially from apartment economics — single-family homes have higher per-unit operating expenses (separate maintenance, separate yard care), longer average tenant tenure (3–5 years vs 1–2 for apartments), and concentrated geographic footprints. The post-2008 emergence of large-scale institutional single-family rental was one of the most significant structural changes in U.S. residential real estate in decades. (3) Manufactured housing REITs — Equity LifeStyle Properties, Sun Communities. Structurally the strongest residential REIT sub-segment because the REIT owns the land beneath the home rather than the home itself; residents own their manufactured homes (depreciating personal property) and pay rent to lease the land plot under them; tenant mobility is structurally low (moving a manufactured home costs $5K–$15K and requires a destination park). Operating margins, occupancy stability, and rent growth have all been structurally strong for two decades. (4) Student housing — American Campus Communities (acquired by Blackstone 2022). The wisdom-tradition references for this industry beyond NAREIT primer material are sparse — Sam Zell's autobiography on apartment operations is the canonical practitioner reference; Howard Marks on cycles applies. The single most useful frame: residential REITs are not a homogeneous category — apartment REITs are mid-cycle housing exposure with one-year leases; single-family rental is a relatively new structural play; manufactured housing is the structurally highest-quality piece because of land-vs-improvement economics.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. NAREIT REIT 101 series. The U.S. Census Bureau Housing Vacancy and Homeownership data. RealPage and Yardi Matrix for apartment-rent data where freely summarized. The National Multifamily Housing Council (NMHC). Sam Zell's autobiography Am I Being Too Subtle? Damodaran's residential-REIT data. Specific industry trade press — Multi-Housing News, GlobeSt, Bisnow.

Length: 3,500–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Residential REITs in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
