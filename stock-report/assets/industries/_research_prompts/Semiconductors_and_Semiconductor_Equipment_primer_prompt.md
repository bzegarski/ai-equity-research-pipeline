What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the SEMICONDUCTORS & SEMICONDUCTOR EQUIPMENT industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the multi-year capex cycle that defines semiconductor economics, the structurally distinct economics of fabless designers (NVIDIA, AMD, Marvell), foundry-and-IDM vertically integrated manufacturers (TSMC, Intel, Samsung), specialty-chip designers (Texas Instruments, Analog Devices, Microchip), and equipment manufacturers (ASML, Applied Materials, KLA, Lam Research). I want to understand the AI capex super-cycle of 2024–25 in this context and have intuition for the canonical bust analogs (1996, 2001, 2008, 2018) that the wisdom tradition treats as required reading.

What to attend to particularly for Semiconductors & Semiconductor Equipment. The industry has four structurally different sub-segments with different capital intensity, cycle exposure, and moat shape. (1) Fabless designers — NVIDIA (the canonical AI-cycle compounder; share price ~30x from 2018 to 2024 driven by data-center GPU dominance), AMD, Marvell Technology, Broadcom (semiconductor segment plus VMware software), Qualcomm (mobile and modem; royalty-revenue exposure to Apple). Fabless designers focus on chip architecture and IP, contract manufacturing to TSMC or Samsung, and earn high gross margins (NVIDIA 70–75%, AMD 50%) with modest capex. (2) Foundries and IDM (integrated device manufacturers) — Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC; the world's leading foundry by far, the structural producer of advanced chips for NVIDIA, AMD, Apple, Qualcomm), Samsung Electronics (Korea-listed; foundry plus memory), Intel (struggling IDM transition with foundry ambitions), GlobalFoundries, SK Hynix (memory). Capital intensity is enormous — a leading-edge fab costs $20–$40B and takes 4–5 years to build. TSMC's capex in 2025 alone was approximately $42B. (3) Specialty / analog chip designers — Texas Instruments (the canonical compounder in analog with disciplined long-cycle capital allocation; multi-decade buyback discipline), Analog Devices, Microchip Technology, NXP Semiconductors, Renesas (Japan-listed), Infineon (Germany-listed). Analog and mixed-signal chips have longer product life-cycles (10–20 years per design vs 18 months for digital), structurally less commoditization, and 60–70% gross margins for the strongest operators. (4) Memory — Micron Technology, SK Hynix, Samsung. Commodity-priced (DRAM and NAND), violently cyclical (the 2018–19 collapse, the 2022–23 collapse, the 2024 partial recovery), capex-heavy. (5) Semiconductor equipment — ASML (Netherlands-listed; the structural monopoly on EUV lithography, the most important single position in the entire semiconductor capital-equipment stack), Applied Materials (broader fab equipment), Lam Research, KLA Corporation (process control and inspection), Tokyo Electron (Japan-listed). The structural advantage of equipment manufacturers: they sell to all foundries and IDMs, take no chip-specific bet, and benefit from the entire industry's capex cycle. ASML's monopoly on EUV (the only company that can make extreme-ultraviolet lithography systems used for sub-7nm node manufacturing) is one of the most thoroughly documented structural moats in modern equity literature. The wisdom-tradition references for this industry are limited from the canonical books — Munger has commented favorably at Daily Journal annual meetings on TSMC's structural position; Buffett briefly held TSMC (purchased 2022, materially trimmed 2023, citing geopolitical risk; the rapid-trim is itself the canonical wisdom-tradition lesson on how to handle a position when the reasoning that justified it shifts). Howard Marks's writing on cyclical industries applies in full. The cycle history (1996 SOX peak and 2001 collapse, 2008 collapse, 2018 cycle, 2022–23 cycle, 2024–25 AI cycle) is essential reading.

Howard Marks on commodity-and-cyclical-industry behavior. Damodaran's semiconductor data. Specific industry trade press — EE Times, Tom's Hardware, AnandTech (for design-and-architecture context), The Information.
