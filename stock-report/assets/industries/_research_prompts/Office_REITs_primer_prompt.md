## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the OFFICE REITs industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for why office REITs have become one of the most distressed corners of U.S. equities in the post-pandemic era, how to distinguish trophy-CBD office (still leasing, premium-rent) from commodity-suburban office (in long-term decline), the magnitude of the structural shift from work-from-home, and the contrarian view (Sam Zell-style "blood in the streets") that has begun to attract capital to high-quality urban office at distressed prices.

What to attend to particularly for Office REITs. The industry has been through one of the most dramatic structural-and-cyclical resets in the history of public real-estate investing. The pre-2020 office-REIT thesis was straightforward: high-quality CBD (central business district) offices in major U.S. metros leased to creditworthy corporate tenants on 7–15 year leases at gradually rising rents. The post-2020 reality has been: hybrid work has reduced office attendance materially (a Stanford / WFH Research consensus suggests 25–35% of workers WFH some or all of the time post-pandemic, vs ~5% pre-pandemic), commodity-suburban-office vacancy has risen to historic levels (15–25% in many markets), and office-loan defaults have begun to flow through commercial-real-estate lenders. The canonical names — Boston Properties (now BXP; the largest pure-play U.S. office REIT, with high-end CBD portfolio in Boston, NY, DC, San Francisco, LA), Vornado Realty Trust (Manhattan-focused), SL Green Realty (Manhattan-focused), Cousins Properties (Sun Belt focus), Highwoods Properties (Sun Belt focus), Kilroy Realty (West Coast focus), Empire State Realty Trust (NYC), Hudson Pacific Properties (Hollywood and tech-heavy West Coast; significantly distressed). International — Brookfield Property Partners' office holdings, Land Securities (UK), British Land. The structural thesis questions worth asking. (1) Where in the rent curve does the portfolio sit — trophy Class A CBD (the best of the best is leasing at rising rents; tenants flight-to-quality has actually compressed vacancy at the very top end of major markets) vs Class B / older CBD (multi-decade replacement-cost economics with significant capex requirements to compete) vs commodity suburban office (long-term decline; meaningful conversion-to-residential candidates). (2) Tenant credit and lease maturity ladder — how many years before significant lease expirations, and how does the office REIT think about renewal probability and rent levels at renewal. (3) Debt structure and covenant exposure — many office REITs entered the pandemic with substantial debt loads at fixed rates that are now refinancing at materially higher rates. The 2024–25 valuation reset has produced 50–80% share-price declines for some office REITs from 2019 highs (BXP, Vornado, SL Green all materially down through 2024 with partial recovery emerging). The contrarian thesis (associated with Sam Zell, Bill Gross, Cohen & Steers, certain value-investor letters) is that high-quality CBD office at distressed valuations now offers asymmetric upside if hybrid-work patterns stabilize and capital costs decline. The wisdom-tradition references for this industry beyond NAREIT primer material are sparse — Howard Marks's writing on cycles applies; Sam Zell's autobiography on opportunistic real-estate investing applies. The single most useful frame: distinguish trophy-CBD office (a structurally-challenged-but-quality real-estate segment that may offer asymmetric upside) from commodity suburban and Class B office (in structural long-term decline regardless of cyclical pricing).

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. NAREIT REIT 101 series. The Stanford / WFH Research data on hybrid-work patterns (Nick Bloom's research is the canonical academic reference). CBRE and JLL office market reports. The Federal Reserve's Senior Loan Officer Survey for commercial real-estate lending standards. Sam Zell's autobiography Am I Being Too Subtle? for the canonical opportunistic real-estate investor voice. Howard Marks on real-estate cycles. Damodaran's office-REIT data. Specific industry trade press — Commercial Observer, Real Estate Weekly.

Length: 3,500–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Office REITs in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
