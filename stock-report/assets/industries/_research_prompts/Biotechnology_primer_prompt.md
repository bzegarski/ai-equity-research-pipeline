## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the BIOTECHNOLOGY industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for why biotech is genuinely option-portfolio investing rather than equity investing in the conventional sense, the FDA approval pipeline as the central determinant of company outcomes, the structural difference between platform-biotech (genuine science-driven moats) and single-product biotech (lottery-ticket structure), and Munger's persistent skepticism about most retail investors' ability to handicap clinical trials.

What to attend to particularly for Biotechnology. The industry's defining feature is option-pricing economics — most biotech pipelines fail (90%+ of phase 1 candidates do not reach approval; ~50% of phase 3 candidates fail), and a few succeed enormously, so portfolio-level outcomes are dominated by tail events. The canonical names span scale and sub-segment. (1) Large-cap diversified biotechs — Vertex Pharmaceuticals (cystic fibrosis franchise, CRISPR-based sickle-cell therapy approved 2023), Regeneron (Eylea franchise, Dupixent partnership with Sanofi), Gilead Sciences (HIV franchise plus oncology), Moderna (post-COVID), BioMarin Pharmaceutical, Alnylam Pharmaceuticals (RNAi platform), Incyte. These are the structurally healthier corner where commercial drugs generate cash flow that funds R&D pipeline. (2) Mid-cap biotechs with one or two approved drugs — Vertex-level scale is rare; most mid-cap biotechs are concentrated in a single franchise (Sarepta in Duchenne muscular dystrophy; Ionis in antisense oligonucleotides; Halozyme; Argenx). (3) Pre-revenue biotechs — hundreds of small-cap names with no approved drug, valuation entirely based on pipeline option value. The vast majority generate negative cash flow indefinitely until either acquisition or approval; most acquire via M&A from large pharma at advanced clinical stages. (4) Specialized therapeutic platforms — Moderna and BioNTech (mRNA platforms; the COVID vaccines validated the platform but commercial sustainability beyond COVID has been mixed); Beam Therapeutics, CRISPR Therapeutics, Editas Medicine (gene-editing); cell therapy companies (Iovance Biotherapeutics, 2seventy bio). (5) Specialty / orphan-disease focus — companies focused on rare diseases benefit from premium pricing under the Orphan Drug Act, FDA priority review, and patient-population concentration that simplifies clinical trials. The wisdom-tradition position is essentially exclusion. Munger has been explicit at Daily Journal annual meetings (cite year) about the difficulty of biotech investing for non-specialists; Buffett has rarely owned biotech stocks. The empirical analytical move when studying biotech: read the FDA Orange Book for patent-and-exclusivity context; read clinicaltrials.gov for trial-stage and primary-endpoint context; understand that the "correct" valuation of a pre-revenue biotech is option-pricing-derived rather than DCF-derived. Damodaran's writing on biotech valuation as option pricing is the canonical academic reference.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. The FDA Orange Book (free; canonical patent-and-exclusivity database). Clinicaltrials.gov for trial registration and status. The BIO (Biotechnology Innovation Organization) reports on industry economics. Damodaran's biotech valuation framework as option pricing. Munger's Daily Journal annual meeting commentary on biotech (cite year). Specific industry trade press — Endpoints News, STAT News (especially Adam Feuerstein's coverage), FierceBiotech, BioPharma Dive.

Length: 3,500–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Biotechnology businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
