## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the CONSTRUCTION & ENGINEERING industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the project-execution risk that is the defining feature of E&C economics, the difference between bid-build and design-build contract structures, why backlog is a leading indicator but a noisy one, and why this industry has produced spectacular blow-ups historically (Westinghouse-CB&I, McDermott).

What to attend to particularly for Construction & Engineering. The industry's defining feature is project-execution risk on multi-year fixed-price (or quasi-fixed-price) contracts. A single mis-estimated mega-project can write off years of profits — the canonical recent examples are Westinghouse / CB&I on AP1000 nuclear projects (2017 bankruptcy), McDermott International on Cameron LNG, and the Bechtel-Bechtel-Bechtel cycle on various fixed-price oil-and-gas projects. Backlog (signed contracts not yet executed) is the leading indicator of revenue but a noisy one because backlog at risky terms can still produce losses. Book-to-bill ratio (new bookings / revenue executed) is the cycle indicator. The canonical names are Quanta Services (electrical infrastructure, the strongest performer of the recent decade), AECOM (professional services skewed toward design rather than build), Fluor, KBR, EMCOR, MasTec, Granite Construction, Dycom, MYR Group. Three contract structures matter and shape the risk profile: bid-build (designer separate; contractor takes execution risk on fixed price), design-build (contractor takes integrated design + execution risk; higher margin if it works), and cost-plus (fee-only structure on government contracts; lowest risk, lowest reward). The composition of the portfolio across these three is one of the central management questions. The structural growth piece is electrical infrastructure (utility transmission and distribution buildout, data-center construction, EV charging), where Quanta has demonstrated the value of long-term framework agreements with regulated utilities. Wisdom-tradition writers have engaged this industry less than most; Howard Marks's writing on cycles and on the hazards of cyclically-leveraged businesses applies. Munger's general skepticism of project-based businesses with execution risk is the right baseline.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. The American Society of Civil Engineers (ASCE) Infrastructure Report Card (free) for U.S. public infrastructure context. ENR (Engineering News-Record) for industry trade press. Damodaran's construction-and-engineering data. The U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis Construction Spending data (free monthly). Specific industry references — Construction Industry Round Table, Associated General Contractors of America.

Length: 3,000–4,500 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Construction & Engineering businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
