## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the AUTOMOBILES & COMPONENTS industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for why the OEM (vehicle manufacturer) business has historically been one of the worst businesses in the public market — high capital intensity, brutal cyclical demand, organized labor capturing surplus, regulatory exposure on every front — and why the auto-parts aftermarket and certain specialty components (semis, batteries) sit alongside as structurally healthier sub-segments.

What to attend to particularly for Automobiles & Components. The industry has three structurally different sub-segments. (1) OEM vehicle manufacturers — Ford, General Motors, Stellantis, Tesla, Toyota, Volkswagen, BYD, Honda, Hyundai-Kia. Capital-intensive (a new vehicle platform costs $1B+ over 4 years; a new factory costs $1B–$3B); cyclical (U.S. light-vehicle sales swing from ~10M units at recession troughs to ~17M at peaks); high fixed costs (UAW labor contracts in the U.S.; equivalent organized labor across most major OEMs); regulatory pressure on emissions, safety, and EV mandates. The 2009 GM bankruptcy and Chrysler bankruptcy are the canonical cautionary tales. The Tesla disruption from 2012 onward, then the BYD scaling from 2020 onward, are the structural reshaping events of the modern era. (2) Auto parts and components — Magna International, Aptiv, BorgWarner, Lear, Gentex, Visteon, Mobileye, Continental, Bosch (private). These vary enormously in moat shape — Gentex's auto-dimming mirror franchise is essentially a category monopoly; Mobileye's ADAS chips are a leading position in a fast-growing category; commodity wiring harness and seat suppliers have weak moat shapes. (3) Auto aftermarket — AutoZone, O'Reilly Automotive, Genuine Parts, Advance Auto Parts, LKQ. These are the structurally healthiest piece — vehicles age 12+ years on average, the installed base of cars needing replacement parts grows steadily, and the retail formats (DIY plus do-it-for-me) operate on density-and-distribution moats. AutoZone and O'Reilly have compounded shareholder returns at 15–20%+ annually for two decades. Wisdom-tradition writers have engaged this industry mixed: Buffett owns Berkshire Auto Group (Van Tuyl, 2014 acquisition; auto retail) and has written favorably on retail-dealership economics; Charlie Munger has long admired Costco's automotive-services attach revenue; Pat Dorsey's Little Book gives the cleanest treatment of why aftermarket retail compounds. The single most useful frame: separate the cyclical, capital-heavy OEM business from the slow-growth, high-quality aftermarket retail business; they are different worlds under one GICS label.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. The U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis Auto and Truck Sales data. Wards Intelligence and IHS Markit / S&P Global Mobility for industry data. The Center for Automotive Research (CAR) for industry analysis. Damodaran's auto-sector data. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on aftermarket retail moats. Buffett's Berkshire Auto Group commentary (2014 letter and onward). Specific industry trade press — Automotive News, Motor Intelligence.

Length: 3,500–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Automobiles & Components businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
