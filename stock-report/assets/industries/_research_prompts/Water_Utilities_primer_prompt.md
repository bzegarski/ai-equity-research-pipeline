## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the WATER UTILITIES industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the highly regulated rate-base / allowed-ROE economics of water utilities, why this is the slowest-growing but lowest-volatility corner of the utility universe, the structural fragmentation of U.S. water utilities (~50,000 distinct water systems, mostly municipally owned, with ongoing privatization-and-consolidation dynamics among the listed publicly-owned operators), and the secular tailwind from aging-infrastructure replacement.

What to attend to particularly for Water Utilities. The industry's defining feature is highly regulated rate-base economics with structurally lower allowed-ROE volatility than electric or gas utilities — water has fewer commodity-pass-through complications, no major fuel-cost cycles, no comparable wildfire-liability exposure, and operationally simpler grid management. The U.S. water industry is structurally fragmented — over 50,000 community water systems exist, the vast majority municipally owned. The listed publicly-owned U.S. water utilities are concentrated in a small group: American Water Works (the largest publicly-owned U.S. water utility by far, with regulated water and wastewater operations across 14 states), Essential Utilities (formerly Aqua America; Pennsylvania-based with regulated water across multiple states), American States Water (California-focused), California Water Service Group, SJW Group, Middlesex Water, Artesian Resources, Pure Water (smaller), Global Water Resources. Internationally — Severn Trent (UK), United Utilities (UK), Veolia (France-listed; mixed water plus waste), Suez (private). The economic mechanics — rate base of regulated water and wastewater infrastructure (treatment plants, distribution mains, storage); rate cases filed every 1–4 years with state public utility commissions; allowed ROEs typically 9.5–10.5%, similar to electric. The structural growth driver — secular replacement of aging U.S. water infrastructure (the EPA estimates $1T+ in needed water-system investment over 20 years; the 2021 Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act allocated $55B specifically for water-system improvements). Privatization-and-consolidation — the publicly-owned operators have grown materially through acquiring small municipally-owned systems, taking advantage of municipalities' inability to fund needed capex; the consolidation trajectory has decades to run given the structural fragmentation. Wisdom-tradition references for this industry are extremely sparse — Buffett has not engaged water utilities directly; Pat Dorsey's Little Book on regulated-monopoly moats applies; Howard Marks's writing on long-duration cash-flow businesses applies. The single most useful frame: water utilities are the slowest-growing but most predictable corner of the regulated-utility universe, with structural growth from infrastructure-replacement capex compounded over decades and a long privatization runway.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. The American Water Works Association (AWWA) for industry-level data. The U.S. EPA's Drinking Water Infrastructure Needs Survey (free; canonical reference for replacement-capex trajectory). The National Association of Water Companies (NAWC) for industry context. NARUC for state-regulatory data. Damodaran's water-utilities data. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on regulated-monopoly moats.

Length: 3,000–4,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Water Utilities businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
