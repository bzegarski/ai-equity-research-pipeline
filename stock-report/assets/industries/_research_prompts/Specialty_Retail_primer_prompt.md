## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the SPECIALTY RETAIL industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the unit-economics framework that defines specialty retail (sales per square foot, four-wall margin, payback period, store-cohort maturation curves), the structurally distinct sub-segments (home improvement, off-price, auto parts, apparel specialty), and how to identify the rare retailer with a durable moat (TJX, Home Depot, AutoZone) vs the much larger universe of fashion-cycle and category-cycle retailers that revert to the mean.

What to attend to particularly for Specialty Retail. The industry breaks into structurally healthy sub-segments and structurally challenged ones, and a single primer voice should distinguish them. (1) Home improvement — Home Depot, Lowe's. Duopoly, scale advantages in distribution and pro-customer relationships, residential-construction and R&R cycle exposure. Buffett-quality returns on capital sustained for two decades. (2) Off-price apparel — TJX (T.J. Maxx, Marshalls, HomeGoods), Ross Stores, Burlington. The structurally healthiest piece of apparel retail because off-price benefits from full-price retailers' inventory mistakes, treasure-hunt store experience drives repeat foot traffic, and online competition is structurally weaker (the off-price assortment changes constantly, defeating algorithmic curation). (3) Auto parts retail — AutoZone, O'Reilly Automotive, Genuine Parts, Advance Auto Parts. Density-and-distribution moats; aging vehicle fleet drives steady demand growth; do-it-yourself plus do-it-for-me dual customer base. AutoZone and O'Reilly have both compounded shareholder returns at 15–20%+ for two decades. (4) Specialty apparel and accessories — Lululemon, Gap, American Eagle, Abercrombie, Urban Outfitters, Foot Locker, Buckle. Fashion-cycle exposed; the structurally challenged piece. Brand strength matters but is rarely durable through a generation. Lululemon's 2010s run is the canonical recent compounder; the 2024–25 Lululemon stumble is the canonical recent reversion. (5) Pet retail — Chewy, Petco, PetSmart (private). Structurally growing category with subscription-model traction. (6) Other specialty — Best Buy (electronics; secularly challenged), Bath & Body Works, Ulta Beauty (cosmetics specialty), DICK'S Sporting Goods, Williams-Sonoma (premium home), Five Below (low-priced specialty), Floor & Decor (flooring). Wisdom-tradition references: Buffett's broader commentary on retail moats (Berkshire owns Ben Bridge Jeweler, Helzberg, Borsheims, See's Candies retail); Pat Dorsey's Little Book on retail moats (especially the chapter that distinguishes density-and-distribution moats from fashion-cycle businesses); Munger on Costco's retail model. The single most useful frame: ask whether the retailer's moat is in the supply chain (off-price, home improvement) or in fashion-cycle brand resonance (apparel specialty); the former compounds steadily, the latter doesn't.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. The U.S. Census Bureau Retail Trade data (free monthly). The National Retail Federation (NRF) for industry-level data. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on retail moats. Buffett's Berkshire commentary on retail (Helzberg, Ben Bridge, See's, the broader retail discussion across letters). Munger's Costco and retail commentary. Damodaran's specialty-retail data. Specific industry trade press — RetailDive, Sourcing Journal, Internet Retailer.

Length: 3,500–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Specialty Retail businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
