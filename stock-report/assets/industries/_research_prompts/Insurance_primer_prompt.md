## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the INSURANCE industry — property and casualty, life, reinsurance, specialty — to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the float concept — the single most important idea the wisdom tradition has produced about insurance — and how it transforms the economics of well-underwritten insurance into "interest-free leverage" that compounds over decades. I should also have intuition for the very different economics of property-and-casualty (P&C) versus life insurance, how reinsurance differs from primary insurance, why specialty and excess-and-surplus (E&S) lines often have stronger structural margins than commodity personal-auto and homeowners insurance, and the canonical Buffett writings on each.

What to attend to particularly for Insurance. Insurance is the deepest territory of the wisdom tradition after Banks. Buffett has written about insurance in every annual Berkshire letter from 1980 onward; the 1976 GEICO internal memo, the 1995 Berkshire purchase of GEICO (already 51% owned; the buyout completed the deal), and the 1996 letter on GEICO's distribution-cost moat are canonical. The economics: an insurer collects premiums up front and pays claims later (sometimes years later for long-tail lines like workers' comp or medical malpractice). The cash held between premium collection and claim payment — the "float" — can be invested. If the underwriting is profitable on average (combined ratio < 100%), the float is held at no cost (or negative cost) and the investment income on the float compounds. That single insight — that well-underwritten insurance is "interest-free leverage" — is what made GEICO and the Berkshire reinsurance operations so valuable. The canonical names span sub-segment and scale. (1) Property & casualty primary — Progressive, Allstate, Travelers, Chubb (specialty plus mid-market), Hartford Financial Services, AIG (still in transition), Liberty Mutual (private mutual), USAA (private). Personal auto and homeowners are the largest commodity-priced lines; specialty lines (workers' comp, professional liability, environmental, marine, energy) earn structurally higher margins. The Geico 1996 letter on distribution-cost moats applies most directly to the primary auto business. (2) Reinsurance — Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group, Munich Re, Swiss Re, Hannover Re, RenaissanceRe, Everest Group, Arch Capital. Reinsurance is structurally cyclical (hard markets follow large catastrophe years; soft markets emerge after 5–8 years of capital accumulation); the Buffett-quality operators in reinsurance are the ones who walk away from underpriced business in soft markets. (3) Life and annuity — MetLife, Prudential Financial, AFLAC, Manulife, Sun Life, Brighthouse, Equitable Holdings, Globe Life. Long-duration liabilities; interest-rate sensitivity; mortality-and-longevity risk; asset-and-liability matching is the central management discipline. (4) Specialty and E&S — W.R. Berkley, Markel Group, Kinsale Capital, Hamilton Insurance Group, Skyward Specialty. Higher-margin niche lines; underwriting expertise rather than scale is the moat. Markel Group is the canonical Berkshire-shape mini-conglomerate that compounds book value through underwriting plus investment portfolio. (5) Title insurance — Fidelity National Financial, First American Financial, Stewart Information Services. Specialty sub-segment with cyclical exposure to home transactions. (6) Insurance distribution / brokers — Marsh McLennan, Aon, Arthur J. Gallagher, Brown & Brown (also classified Financial Services). The wisdom-tradition writings center on the Buffett-Berkshire body — GEICO, the float doctrine, the reinsurance discipline, the National Indemnity acquisition — plus Howard Marks's commentary on insurance-cycle behavior and Markel's annual letters as the closest practitioner-following-Buffett body of writing.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Buffett's GEICO 1976 internal memo, 1995 acquisition letter, 1996 distribution-moat letter, plus the multi-decade float-and-underwriting commentary across letters. The 1976–2024 body of Berkshire insurance writing is the deepest practitioner literature on insurance economics. Howard Marks on insurance-cycle behavior. Markel Group's annual letters as canonical contemporary practitioner writing. The NAIC for U.S. insurance regulatory data. The S&P Global / A.M. Best Industry Reports for solvency and rating context. Damodaran's insurance-sector data.

Length: 5,000–7,000 words (Insurance earns the longest length because the wisdom-tradition body is deepest and the sub-segments most distinct). Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Insurance businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
