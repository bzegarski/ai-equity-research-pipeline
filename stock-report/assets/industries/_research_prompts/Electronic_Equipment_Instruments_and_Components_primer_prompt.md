## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the ELECTRONIC EQUIPMENT, INSTRUMENTS & COMPONENTS industry — test and measurement, electronic components and connectors, electronic manufacturing services — to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the test-and-measurement and electronic-components sub-segments that constitute much of this industry, the customer-line tooling switching costs at strong component franchises (TE Connectivity, Amphenol), and the electronic manufacturing services (EMS) sub-segment dynamics that have produced the 2024–25 AI-server boom for Hon Hai (Foxconn) and lower-margin contract manufacturers.

What to attend to particularly for Electronic Equipment, Instruments & Components. This industry sits between Communications Equipment and Semiconductors and is genuinely several different business types. (1) Test and measurement equipment — Keysight Technologies, Teradyne, Cognex, Mettler-Toledo (industrial precision instruments), Trimble (positioning and survey equipment). The Mettler-Toledo case is the canonical compounder in this sub-segment — selling lab balances, scales, and analytical instruments to pharma, food, chemicals, and industrial labs at high margins, with recurring service-and-consumables revenue. Operating margins of 25–30% sustained for two decades. (2) Electronic components and connectors — TE Connectivity, Amphenol, Sensata Technologies, Vishay Intertechnology, AVX (acquired by Kyocera), Molex (private). Customer-line tooling switching costs — once an electronic component is designed into a customer's product, the cost of replacing it at the customer's manufacturing line is high enough that suppliers earn long-cycle relationships. Amphenol is the canonical case with multi-decade compounding through disciplined small-acquisition roll-up plus organic growth. (3) Electronic manufacturing services (EMS) — Hon Hai Precision Industry / Foxconn (Taiwan-listed; the world's largest EMS by far, primary Apple iPhone assembler, major NVIDIA AI-server assembler), Jabil Circuit, Flex (formerly Flextronics), Celestica, Sanmina, Benchmark Electronics. Razor-thin margins (1–4%), capital-intensive, with rare exceptions where specialization (Foxconn's iPhone assembly scale; Super Micro's AI-server design-build hybrid) produces higher margins. (4) Industrial sensors and components — Sensata, Watts Water Technologies (sensors for water industry), Roper Technologies (also Industrial Conglomerates). (5) Specialty electronic instrumentation — Bio-Rad Laboratories (also Health Care Equipment), Mettler-Toledo, Itron (smart-meter infrastructure). The wisdom-tradition references for this industry are minimal. Buffett's broader thinking on niche-leader industrial businesses (Marmon 2008, ISCAR 2006) applies to specialty component franchises. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on switching-cost moats with electronic-component examples is directly applicable. The single most useful frame: distinguish the high-quality specialty franchises (Mettler-Toledo, Amphenol, TE Connectivity) — which earn structural switching-cost moats and compound steadily — from the EMS commodity manufacturers (Jabil, Flex, Celestica) — which earn thin margins and only occasionally benefit from cycle inflections like the 2024–25 AI server build-out.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Damodaran's electronic-equipment data. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on switching-cost moats with worked component-supplier examples. Mettler-Toledo and Amphenol investor presentations as canonical primer material on the strongest specialty franchises. The IPC (Association Connecting Electronics Industries) for U.S. industry data. SemiAnalysis for AI-server contract-manufacturing context. Specific industry trade press — Electronic Design, EDN, EE Times.

Length: 3,000–4,500 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Electronic Equipment, Instruments & Components businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
