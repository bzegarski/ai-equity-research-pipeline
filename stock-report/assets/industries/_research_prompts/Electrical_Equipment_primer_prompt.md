## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the ELECTRICAL EQUIPMENT industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for how electrical-equipment companies sit at the intersection of the electrification mega-trend and traditional industrial automation, what drives their long-cycle exposure to capital spending, how aftermarket service revenue produces a more stable layer beneath equipment sales, and why this is one of the more durable corners of Industrials.

What to attend to particularly for Electrical Equipment. The industry has two structural tailwinds that distinguish it from generic Industrials. (1) Electrification — the energy transition is requiring grid expansion, transmission and distribution upgrades, EV-charging infrastructure, building electrification, and data-center power management on a scale unseen since the post-WWII grid buildout. The companies positioned to supply that demand — Eaton (power management), Hubbell (electrical products), AMETEK (electronic instruments and electromechanical), Generac (backup power), Schneider Electric (Europe-listed), Siemens Energy — have visible decade-long order growth. (2) Industrial automation — Emerson Electric, Rockwell Automation, ABB (Europe-listed), Honeywell — sells programmable controllers, instrumentation, motors, drives, and software into manufacturing customers whose own capex cycles drive demand. Aftermarket service revenue is the durable layer underneath both — once an electrical-equipment company has shipped 10,000 motor drives or 50 substation transformers, the maintenance, parts, and service revenue compounds for 20–30 years. The wisdom-tradition writings here are sparse; Buffett's Marmon (2008 letter) and ISCAR (2006 acquisition) commentary touch on niche-leader industrial businesses with similar economics. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on switching-cost moats applies — replacing a Rockwell Automation control system on a chemical plant requires hundreds of hours of recommissioning. Capital intensity is moderate; gross margins run 30–40% for the strongest operators; ROIC at the leaders runs 15–25% sustained. The single most informative number for cycle reading is the order-book book-to-bill ratio quarter by quarter; a flip from above 1.0× to below 1.0× signals the leading edge of a slowdown. Geographic mix matters because European industrial spending and U.S. industrial spending sometimes move on different cycles.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. The Edison Electric Institute and NEMA (National Electrical Manufacturers Association) for U.S. industry-level data. The IEA's World Energy Investment annual report for grid-investment trajectories. The U.S. Census Manufacturers' Shipments, Inventories, and Orders data (free monthly). Damodaran's electrical-equipment data. Pat Dorsey on switching-cost moats. Specific industry trade press — T&D World, Electrical Construction & Maintenance, Automation World.

Length: 3,000–4,500 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Electrical Equipment businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
