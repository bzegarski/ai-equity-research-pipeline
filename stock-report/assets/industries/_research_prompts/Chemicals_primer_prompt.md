## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the CHEMICALS industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for how the three very different sub-segments of Chemicals actually work — commodity petrochemicals, specialty chemicals, and industrial gases — and why a single primer voice has to acknowledge them separately.

What to attend to particularly for Chemicals. The single most important distinction is commodity vs specialty vs industrial gas. (1) Commodity petrochemicals — Dow, LyondellBasell, Westlake, Olin, Celanese — are price-takers on outputs (ethylene, propylene, polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC) and price-takers on inputs (ethane, naphtha, natural gas). Capacity utilization swings on demand cycles and on capacity additions; the Asian, U.S. Gulf Coast, and Middle East cracker buildouts arrive in waves and depress global margins for years at a time. The math is brutal: a 5% utilization drop typically translates to a 30–50% EBITDA decline. The U.S. ethane advantage (cheap natural-gas-liquids feedstock vs naphtha-cracker peers in Europe and Asia) is structural and load-bearing. (2) Specialty chemicals — Sherwin-Williams (the canonical specialty case in coatings; long-running Buffett-quality returns on capital), RPM International, PPG, Ecolab (water and process chemistry), Air Products / Linde / Air Liquide (industrial gases — see below), International Flavors & Fragrances, Givaudan, Ashland, Lubrizol (Berkshire's 2011 acquisition; Buffett's wisdom-tradition specialty reference). Specialty earns durable margins because formulation expertise creates switching costs at the customer's process line — replacing a coating in an automotive paint shop or a flavor in a food line involves requalification cycles measured in months. (3) Industrial gases — Linde, Air Liquide, Air Products — are a global oligopoly with on-site supply contracts that produce utility-style regulated returns over 15–20-year contracts; this is the most attractive corner of Chemicals from a moat-durability standpoint. The wisdom-tradition writings closest to dispositive are Buffett's Lubrizol commentary and Munger's general remarks on specialty chemicals as one of the better corners of Materials. Capex intensity is high industry-wide; cycles are long.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. American Chemistry Council for U.S. industry data. CEFIC (European Chemical Industry Council) for European data. ICIS and Chemical Week for industry trade press. Buffett's Lubrizol acquisition commentary. Damodaran's chemicals data. Pat Dorsey on specialty-chemical moats. The IHS Markit / S&P Global Petrochemicals data where freely summarized.

Length: 3,000–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Chemicals businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
