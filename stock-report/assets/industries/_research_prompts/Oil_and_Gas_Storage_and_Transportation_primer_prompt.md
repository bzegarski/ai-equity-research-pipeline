## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the OIL & GAS STORAGE & TRANSPORTATION (MIDSTREAM) industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for how a midstream business actually works — the toll-road economics, the take-or-pay contract structure, the distribution-coverage discipline, the MLP-to-C-corp transition history, and the historical patterns of leverage and capital discipline that have defined the industry's last fifteen years.

What to attend to particularly for Oil & Gas Storage & Transportation. Midstream is the most utility-like piece of the energy value chain. Pipelines, storage tanks, gathering systems, fractionators, and export terminals earn fees on volume rather than on price — when oil falls 50%, midstream cash flow typically falls 5–15%. Take-or-pay contracts (the customer commits to pay for capacity whether or not it ships product) and minimum-volume commitments insulate further. The canonical names are Enterprise Products Partners, Energy Transfer, Kinder Morgan, Williams Companies, ONEOK, Pembina, MPLX, Targa Resources. Three numbers matter most. (1) Distribution coverage ratio = distributable cash flow / distributions paid; aim for >1.1× through the cycle. (2) Debt / EBITDA — historically aggressive at 5–6× during the 2010s MLP boom, deleveraged toward 3–4× through 2017–2024. (3) Growth capex / maintenance capex — a midstream company growing capex base is creating value if returns exceed cost of capital and destroying it if not. The 2014–16 commodity collapse exposed which midstream operators had over-leveraged into volume assumptions that didn't hold; the 2017–2020 wave of MLP-to-C-corp simplifications (Enterprise Products kept its MLP structure; many peers converted) reset the tax-and-distribution economics. FERC regulates interstate pipeline tariffs, which adds a regulated-return floor to a meaningful share of EBITDA at most large pipeline companies. Wisdom-tradition writers have engaged midstream less than other energy pieces; Howard Marks's writing on toll-road and infrastructure businesses applies. The single best framing: midstream is closer to a regulated utility than to upstream — the cycle question is the financing cycle, not the commodity cycle.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) for interstate pipeline tariff filings and the regulatory framework. The EIA's pipeline and natural-gas storage data. The Energy Infrastructure Council (formerly MLP Association) for industry-level commentary. Howard Marks on cycles in infrastructure businesses. Damodaran's data on regulated-cash-flow businesses. RBN Energy's Daily Blog for midstream-specific commentary. Hinds Howard's Energy Income Investor and similar contemporary midstream-specialist writers.

Length: 3,000–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Oil & Gas Storage & Transportation businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
