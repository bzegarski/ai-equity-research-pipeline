## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the HOUSEHOLD & PERSONAL PRODUCTS industry — Procter & Gamble, Colgate, household cleaning products, beauty and personal care — to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the brand-and-distribution moats that defined the historic compounding of P&G and Colgate, why direct-to-consumer challengers (Dollar Shave Club, Harry's, Native, Olaplex, Drunk Elephant) reshaped the industry through the 2010s, the structurally distinct economics of luxury beauty (Estée Lauder, L'Oréal), and the broader ESG and reformulation pressures across household cleaning and personal care.

What to attend to particularly for Household & Personal Products. The industry has three structurally different sub-segments. (1) Mass-market household and personal-care brands — Procter & Gamble, Colgate-Palmolive, Kimberly-Clark, Reckitt Benckiser, Unilever (UK / Netherlands listed), Church & Dwight, Clorox, Energizer, Edgewell. The historic moats — brand awareness compounded over decades, retailer-shelf-space negotiating power, advertising-spend scale advantages — held strongly through the 2000s but eroded materially in the 2010s as direct-to-consumer brands (Dollar Shave Club acquired by Unilever 2016, Harry's, Native deodorant acquired by P&G 2017, Olaplex) reached customers through Amazon, Instagram, and DTC websites without paying retailer slotting fees. The strongest mass-market operators have responded with their own DTC initiatives, brand acquisitions of digital natives, and SKU rationalization. The 2010s decade saw operating margin compression at the largest names; the 2020–24 reflation environment saw partial recovery on input-cost-pass-through pricing. (2) Premium beauty and skincare — Estée Lauder, L'Oréal (Paris-listed), Coty, Inter Parfums, Edgewell. Premium beauty has structurally healthier margins (Estée Lauder operating margins 18–22% in good years vs P&G's ~20%) but more cyclical demand tied to discretionary luxury spending and to specific channel exposures (department-store decline from 2010, travel-retail collapse in 2020 and 2024 China travel-retail squeeze). (3) Specialty / niche household — WD-40, Spectrum Brands, Energizer, Central Garden & Pet, Helen of Troy. Smaller universe; brand-driven niche positioning. Wisdom-tradition references: Buffett's broader commentary on consumer franchises (Coca-Cola 1988, See's, the 2007 letter on durable franchises) applies; Buffett's Berkshire owns or has owned Gillette before its 2005 P&G acquisition (commentary across letters); Buffett's Pampered Chef (2002 acquisition) and the broader Berkshire consumer-brand portfolio. Pat Dorsey's Little Book chapter on brand moats with worked examples of Colgate / P&G / Energizer is the canonical primer-level treatment. The single best frame: the durable-brand premium that P&G, Colgate, and Estée Lauder have compounded for decades is real but no longer absolute; understanding which brands genuinely enjoy pricing power in each category vs which face private-label or DTC competition is the central analytical move.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Buffett's commentary on consumer-brand franchises across Berkshire letters (Coca-Cola, See's, Gillette pre-2005, Pampered Chef). NielsenIQ / Circana scanner data where freely available. The Personal Care Products Council (PCPC) for industry data. The Household & Commercial Products Association (HCPA). Damodaran's CPG and beauty sector data. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on brand moats in CPG.

Length: 3,500–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Household & Personal Products businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
