## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the AEROSPACE & DEFENSE industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the duopoly economics of commercial aerospace, the long-cycle program-of-record economics of defense, the unusually deep switching-cost moats at tier-1 suppliers, and how the long government-contract cycle shapes both reported earnings and the politics of defense procurement.

What to attend to particularly for Aerospace & Defense. The industry breaks cleanly into two halves with very different economics. (1) Commercial aerospace is a global duopoly between Boeing and Airbus at the airframe level (with Embraer and Bombardier as smaller regional jet players, and COMAC trying to enter from China). The duopoly produces ~20-year backlogs and pricing power against a fragmented airline customer base. The cautionary lesson is that commercial aerospace returns are catastrophically vulnerable to reputational and safety failures — the Boeing 737 MAX grounding (2019), the multiple 787 quality-control episodes, and the broader 2024 Boeing-quality crisis are the canonical recent stress test of how a duopolist can damage itself. (2) Defense — Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman, Raytheon Technologies (RTX), General Dynamics, BAE Systems, L3Harris — operates on long-term Department of Defense contracts (Programs of Record like F-35, Columbia-class submarines, Sentinel ICBM, Ground Based Strategic Deterrent), which produce 10–20 year revenue visibility once awarded. The five-prime structure has consolidated since the 1990s; entry is essentially closed. (3) Tier-1 suppliers (Heico, Transdigm, Howmet Aerospace, GE Aerospace, Pratt & Whitney inside RTX, Safran, Rolls-Royce) own switching costs as deep as software's: replacing a certified component on an in-service aircraft requires FAA recertification cycles measured in years. Buffett's Precision Castparts acquisition (2015 for $37B) is the canonical wisdom-tradition reference for tier-1 economics, including the cautionary commentary in subsequent letters about the 737 MAX and pandemic disruptions. FlightSafety (Berkshire 1996 acquisition; 2007 letter on capital intensity in training simulators) is a related reference. The single number that matters most: backlog / annual revenue (multi-year visibility) is the leading indicator of revenue stability, while program-margin progression on a fixed-price defense contract is the leading indicator of execution risk. The regulatory / political overlay (defense budget cycles, ITAR export controls, congressional appropriations) is real and asymmetric — it changes the timing of revenue more than the level.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Buffett's Precision Castparts and FlightSafety commentary across Berkshire letters (2015 onward; cite year). The Aerospace Industries Association (AIA) for industry-level data. The U.S. Department of Defense's annual budget request and Selected Acquisition Reports (SARs). The Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI) for global defense-spending data. Aviation Week & Space Technology for trade press. Damodaran's aerospace/defense data. Specific industry references — Forecast International, Teal Group.

Length: 3,500–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Aerospace & Defense businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
