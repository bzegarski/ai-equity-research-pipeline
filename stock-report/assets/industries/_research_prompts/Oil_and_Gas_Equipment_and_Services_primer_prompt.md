## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the OIL & GAS EQUIPMENT & SERVICES industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for how an oilfield-services business actually works — the activity-leverage economics, the distinction between technology-differentiated services and commodity hydraulic-fracturing capacity, the day-rate cycles for drilling rigs and pressure pumping equipment, and why this is the cyclically extreme corner of the energy stack.

What to attend to particularly for Oil & Gas Equipment & Services. This is the most cyclically leveraged industry inside Energy. When E&P companies cut capex 50%, services revenue can drop 40–60%, and services EBITDA can drop 70–90% because the cost base is fixed in the short run (rig leases, equipment depreciation, base headcount). The canonical names are Schlumberger, Halliburton, Baker Hughes (the "big three"), NOV (drilling equipment), ChampionX (artificial lift), Liberty Energy and Patterson-UTI (pressure pumping). The technology-differentiated layer — Schlumberger's reservoir engineering, Halliburton's stimulation chemistry, Baker Hughes's industrial gas turbines through their LNG-equipment business — has materially higher margins than commodity pressure pumping or drilling rig leasing. Day rates and rig counts (the Baker Hughes Rig Count, free weekly) are the leading indicators. The 2014–16 shale-glut collapse and the 2020 pandemic collapse are the canonical recent stress tests; service-company stocks fell 70–90% in each, and several major operators entered Chapter 11. The "picks and shovels" frame applies but cyclically extreme — when activity is low for years, even the best service companies generate negative free cash flow. Capital allocation discipline is the management question and is unusually load-bearing because the temptation to deploy capital at the cycle peak is enormous. Wisdom-tradition writers have engaged services less than upstream or integrateds; Howard Marks on commodity cycles applies directly. Munger's general skepticism of cyclical capital-intensive businesses is the cautionary frame. The international vs U.S. split matters — international rig and offshore activity tracks longer cycles and is dominated by the big three.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Baker Hughes Rig Count (free weekly, the canonical activity indicator). Spears & Associates and Rystad Energy for industry-level activity data where freely summarized. Howard Marks on commodity-services cycles. The EIA's Today in Energy. Specific industry references — World Oil, Hart Energy, Reuters energy desk.

Length: 3,000–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Oil & Gas Equipment & Services businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
