## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the CONSTRUCTION MATERIALS industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for why aggregates and ready-mix concrete are the wisdom-tradition exception case in Materials — businesses with genuine local-monopoly moats inside a sector otherwise dominated by commodity economics — and what specifically the moat depends on.

What to attend to particularly for Construction Materials. The defining feature is the weight-to-value ratio of the products. Aggregates (crushed stone, sand, gravel) sell for $10–$15 per ton at the quarry; trucking aggregates more than 20–50 miles is uneconomic because freight costs more than the product. The result is regional monopolies — a quarry with the right reserves and the right zoning permits within trucking distance of a major construction market is genuinely irreplaceable, because new quarries cannot be permitted easily anywhere near existing population centers. Vulcan Materials and Martin Marietta own the largest U.S. aggregates portfolios; Eagle Materials, Summit Materials, and Heidelberg Materials are major players; CRH (the Irish-listed conglomerate) is the largest by global revenue. Pat Dorsey's Little Book treatment of regional-monopoly moats applies almost perfectly here. Ready-mix concrete shares the regional logic — concrete loses workability after 90 minutes, so concrete plants serve a 30–50-mile radius. Cement is more capex-intensive (a cement plant costs $400M–$1B and takes 4 years to build) and follows similar regional logic at a longer trucking radius. Asphalt is similar to ready-mix in time-and-distance economics. Long-cycle infrastructure spending exposure is the dominant secular driver — federal highway bills (the IIJA $1.2T 2021), state and local construction, and residential construction together drive demand. The canonical wisdom-tradition reference is Charlie Munger's commentary on regional monopolies generally; Buffett has not written extensively about Construction Materials specifically. Pricing power is real and quietly impressive — aggregates have raised prices 3–5% per year for two decades against a commodity-like input. The moat erodes only if zoning permits become easier to obtain (rare) or if alternative materials displace the product (also rare for aggregates).

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. The National Stone, Sand & Gravel Association (NSSGA) for industry-level data. The Portland Cement Association. The U.S. Census Bureau Construction Spending data (free monthly). The American Road and Transportation Builders Association. Pat Dorsey's Little Book That Builds Wealth chapter on regional-monopoly moats. Damodaran's data on construction materials. Vulcan Materials and Martin Marietta investor presentations as canonical primer material on aggregates economics.

Length: 3,000–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Construction Materials businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
