## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the ELECTRIC UTILITIES industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the regulated rate-base / allowed-ROE mechanic that defines electric-utility economics, why understanding this single concept demystifies most of what differs between utilities and other businesses, the present capex super-cycle for grid modernization and decarbonization, and Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway Energy commentary as the canonical wisdom-tradition writeup of this industry.

What to attend to particularly for Electric Utilities. The defining concept is allowed return on rate base, and a primer needs to teach it explicitly. State public utility commissions decide what assets are eligible for inclusion in rate base (typically generation plants the utility owns, transmission and distribution infrastructure, certain regulatory assets), what the allowed return on equity is (typically 9–10.5% currently), and what the cost-of-debt and equity-mix is in the regulated capital structure. The utility's job is to invest in approved assets at the allowed return; the investor's job is to assess whether management can grow rate base at the allowed return without falling into rate disallowances or fuel-cost-recovery mismatches. The canonical names — Duke Energy, Southern Company, NextEra Energy (the canonical recent compounder via aggressive renewables expansion), Dominion Energy, American Electric Power, Exelon, Xcel Energy, Eversource, Edison International, FirstEnergy, Entergy, PPL, Public Service Enterprise Group, CenterPoint, Sempra Energy. NextEra is the most-studied of the recent decade — its Florida Power & Light regulated utility plus its NextEra Energy Resources renewables-development arm produced multi-decade compounding above the industry. Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway Energy (acquired 1999 as MidAmerican Energy; the 2007, 2014, 2017, 2022 letters discuss the business; the 2022 letter on utility capex demands and the energy-transition opportunity is particularly load-bearing) is the canonical wisdom-tradition writeup of utility economics. The five operational questions any utility writeup needs to answer. (1) What is rate-base growth — typically 5–8% annually for the strongest investor-owned utilities; this drives EPS growth at roughly the same rate. (2) Earned ROE vs allowed ROE — the strongest operators consistently earn close to their allowed return; weaker operators leak through fuel-cost mismatches or rate-case denials. (3) Capex / depreciation ratio — > 1× means rate base is growing; the strongest utilities run 1.5–2× during capex super-cycles. (4) FFO / debt — a credit metric; the strongest utilities run FFO/debt above 18–20%, weaker ones below 15% which can pressure credit ratings. (5) Dividend payout ratio — typically 60–75% of GAAP earnings; very high payouts (above 80%) constrain growth investment. The structural risks worth understanding — rate-case denial (a state PUC denying full recovery of capex spent), wildfire liability (the PG&E 2017–19 Camp Fire bankruptcy is the canonical cautionary case), storm-cost disallowances, generation-asset stranding under decarbonization rules. The 2022 rate-cycle re-rating — utilities re-rated downward as long-rates rose because of bond-proxy substitution — and the 2024–25 partial recovery is the present cycle context.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway Energy commentary across Berkshire letters (1999 acquisition reference, 2007, 2014, 2017, 2022, and onward — cite each year). Edison Electric Institute (eei.org) for primer-level material on rate-case mechanics and ROE-on-rate-base. NERC for grid reliability standards. The EIA Electric Power Monthly. NARUC (National Association of Regulatory Utility Commissioners) for the regulatory landscape state-by-state. Damodaran's electric-utilities data. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on regulated-monopoly moats. The PG&E bankruptcy case studies for the climate-liability cautionary tale.

Length: 4,000–5,500 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Electric Utilities businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
