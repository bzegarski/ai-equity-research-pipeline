## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the CONTAINERS & PACKAGING industry to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this industry?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for the customer-line tooling switching costs that make some packaging businesses surprisingly sticky, the substrate-by-substrate economic differences (aluminum vs glass vs plastic vs corrugated), and the secular trends (sustainability, e-commerce, plastic-to-paper substitution) that are reshaping demand.

What to attend to particularly for Containers & Packaging. The substrate divide is the right organizing frame. (1) Metal cans — Ball Corp, Crown Holdings, Ardagh — is a global oligopoly (3–4 players in aluminum cans), with high customer-line tooling switching costs (a beverage filling line is built for specific can dimensions and lid specifications). Demand is structurally growing as aluminum substitutes for plastic in beverages on sustainability grounds. (2) Glass — O-I Glass — is a slower-growth substrate with high energy intensity and tougher trucking economics (heavier than aluminum). (3) Plastic packaging — Berry Global, Sealed Air, Sonoco rigid — is the most fragmented and most exposed to anti-plastic regulatory pressure. (4) Corrugated (cardboard boxes) — International Paper, Packaging Corp of America (PCA), WestRock (now part of Smurfit), Cascades — is structurally tied to e-commerce growth and to consumer/industrial output, with linerboard prices (the input feedstock) driving most of the cycle. The corrugated industry consolidated meaningfully through 2018–24 (Westrock-Smurfit, multiple smaller deals), tightening supply discipline. (5) Specialty / protective packaging — Sealed Air's Bubble Wrap and protective foams — sits closer to specialty chemicals in moat shape. Customer-line tooling switching costs operate at the same logical level as software switching costs but in physical equipment terms — a beverage company qualifying a new can supplier runs trial production lines for 3–6 months before a full conversion. Capacity additions arrive in waves and depress margins; the corrugated industry's 2018 capacity addition wave is the canonical recent example. Charlie Munger has commented favorably on Coca-Cola's bottler relationships (which run through these packaging suppliers); broader wisdom-tradition writing is sparse. Pat Dorsey's framing on switching-cost moats applies. Capex intensity varies by substrate (corrugated mid; metal cans high; glass high).

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs. Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose.

Source preferences. AF&PA (American Forest & Paper Association) for paper and corrugated data. The Can Manufacturers Institute for metal-can data. The Glass Packaging Institute. RISI's Packaging Resin Markets summaries. Damodaran's packaging-industry data. Pat Dorsey's Little Book on switching-cost moats. Specific industry trade press — Packaging Digest, Beverage Industry, Resource Recycling.

Length: 3,000–5,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Containers & Packaging businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
