## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the UTILITIES sector to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this sector?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. I read company writeups across every GICS sector, and I am green on most of them. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses. I want a sector primer that does the same.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for how a Utilities business actually works — most importantly, that it is fundamentally a regulated rate-of-return business where understanding the rate-base mechanics is more important than understanding the income statement. Utilities trade off capital-allocation discipline for regulated certainty; the dollar of capital that earns 9–10% on rate base is more valuable than the dollar on the same income statement that doesn't, and it is the regulator who decides which is which.

What to attend to particularly for Utilities. (a) The defining concept is allowed return on rate base — state public utility commissions decide what regulated assets are eligible for inclusion in rate base, what the allowed return on equity is, and what the cost-of-debt and equity-mix is. The utility's job is to invest in approved assets at the allowed return. The investor's job is to assess whether management can grow rate base at the allowed return without falling into rate disallowances or fuel-cost-recovery mismatches. (b) Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway Energy (originally MidAmerican Energy, acquired 1999) is the wisdom-tradition's deepest engagement with utilities — multiple letters (1999 deal, 2007, 2014, 2017, 2022) discuss what makes this kind of business attractive to a long-term private owner: predictable cash flow, large reinvestment runway at acceptable returns, regulatory protection from competition. The 2022 letter's commentary on utility capex demands and the energy-transition opportunity is particularly load-bearing. (c) The recent capex super-cycle for grid modernization, electrification, and renewables build-out is the dominant present story — utilities have the largest acceptable-return reinvestment runway they've had in fifty years, but execution risk and regulatory-recovery risk both rise with capex intensity. (d) Long-cycle interest-rate sensitivity — utilities trade as bond proxies in many cycles; the 2022 rate-spike re-rating and the 2024–25 partial recovery is the canonical recent example. (e) Sub-industry economics differ — regulated electric utilities (the largest piece), regulated gas distribution (smaller, similar), water utilities (highly regulated, slow-growth), independent power producers / merchant generation (NOT regulated, commodity-priced output), renewables developers (mixed regulated and merchant exposure).

The structure I want. Open with the rate-base / allowed-ROE mechanic and walk through it carefully — this is the single concept that demystifies the rest of the sector. Use Berkshire Hathaway Energy and one or two pure-play regulated utilities (Southern Co., Duke Energy, NextEra) as the worked examples. Walk the metrics that matter (no more than 6, each defined inline using a real-company number — rate base growth, allowed ROE, earned ROE vs allowed, capex / depreciation ratio, FFO / debt, dividend payout ratio). Then the typical risks (rate-case denial, fuel-cost or storm-cost disallowances, capex execution risk on multi-year builds, the long-cycle interest-rate sensitivity, climate / wildfire liability — the PG&E case is the canonical lesson). Then recurring historical patterns (the 1980s nuclear-cost-overrun era, the 2000–02 California energy crisis and Enron-related disruption, the 2017+ wildfire era for Western utilities, the 2022 rate-cycle re-rating). Then the GICS sub-industries with one paragraph each: Electric Utilities, Gas Utilities, Multi-Utilities, Water Utilities, Independent Power and Renewable Electricity Producers.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs (Buffett on See's; Marks on 2007–08). Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose. ESG ratings imported from third-party scorers without engaging what they mean for the unit economics.

Source preferences. Buffett's letters covering Berkshire Hathaway Energy / MidAmerican (1999 deal, 2007, 2014, 2017, 2022, and onward — cite by year). Edison Electric Institute (eei.org) — primer-level material on rate-case mechanics and ROE-on-rate-base. NERC for grid reliability standards. NARUC (National Association of Regulatory Utility Commissioners) for the regulatory landscape state-by-state. The EIA's Electric Power Monthly. Damodaran's utilities sector data. Pat Dorsey's Little Book chapter on regulated-monopoly moats. Howard Marks's commentary on bond-proxy cycles and on cycles in regulated-cash-flow businesses. The PG&E bankruptcy case studies for the climate-liability cautionary tale.

Length: 4,000–6,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Utilities businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
