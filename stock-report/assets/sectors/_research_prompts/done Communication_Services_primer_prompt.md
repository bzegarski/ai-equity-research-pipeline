## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the COMMUNICATION SERVICES sector to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this sector?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. I read company writeups across every GICS sector, and I am green on most of them. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses. I want a sector primer that does the same.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for how a Communication Services business actually works — and this sector is genuinely two very different worlds under one GICS label. The legacy half is telecommunications and traditional media (capex-heavy infrastructure businesses, regulated returns, declining audience for cable / broadcast / print). The internet half is the planet's most profitable business model — ad-supported platforms (Alphabet, Meta), subscription streaming (Netflix), and gaming (EA, Take-Two, Roblox), where network effects and scale economics produce return-on-tangible-capital that the rest of the sector universe cannot match.

What to attend to particularly for Communication Services. (a) The Washington Post (1973–onward) is the canonical Buffett writeup of a media business — the 1973 purchase, the multi-decade hold, the 2014 sale as the local-newspaper economics finally broke. Pull on this writing to teach how Buffett thought about media moats and how he watched them erode. (b) The Buffalo News (1977 acquisition) is the parallel cautionary lesson on small-newspaper economics. (c) Verizon (2020 brief Berkshire position, then exit) is a recent Buffett engagement with telecom that informs how the wisdom tradition reads telecom-style cash-flow profiles. (d) For internet platforms, the wisdom-tradition writers are mostly absent (Munger has been skeptical; Buffett admitted he missed Google publicly), so the contemporary practitioners do the heavy lifting — Stratechery's aggregation theory, Mauboussin on the math of growth and intangibles, Pat Dorsey on network-effect moats. (e) The cord-cutting / streaming-fragmentation / linear-TV-decline cycle is the dominant secular narrative in legacy media. (f) Ad cycles and ad-cycle exposure differ across platform types — search advertising (less cyclical, higher-intent), display and video advertising (more cyclical, brand-budget exposed), classifieds and local advertising (the dying piece).

The structure I want. Open with the bifurcation between legacy infrastructure / media and internet platforms, and treat them as essentially separate primers within one document. For legacy: telecom (rate-of-return economics, capex super-cycles for fiber and 5G, declining wireline dynamics), cable (cord-cutting trajectory, broadband as the durable business inside the cable bundle), broadcast (ad cycles, retransmission consent dynamics), publishing (the local-newspaper collapse, the niche-publication survival pattern). For internet: search advertising, social-network advertising, streaming, gaming. Walk the metrics that matter for each (subscriber ARPU and churn for telecom; cost-per-acquisition for streaming; daily active users and time-spent and ad load for social platforms; gross-bookings and content cost ratio for gaming). Use Buffett's Washington Post and Buffalo News writings as worked examples for legacy media; use Stratechery's aggregation-theory writeups for internet platforms. Then the typical risks (cord-cutting trajectory, regulatory pressure on Big Tech, ad-cycle exposure, content-cost inflation, network-build capex returning sub-cost-of-capital). Then recurring historical patterns (the 1990s cable consolidation, the 2000–02 dot-com correction in advertising, the 2008–10 newspaper collapse, the 2020 streaming surge and 2022 reset). Then the GICS sub-industries with one paragraph each: Diversified Telecommunication Services, Wireless Telecommunication Services, Media (the cable/broadcast/print family), Entertainment (gaming and content production), Interactive Media & Services (the internet-platform family).

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs (Buffett on See's; Marks on 2007–08). Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose. ESG ratings imported from third-party scorers without engaging what they mean for the unit economics.

Source preferences. Buffett's Washington Post writing (1973 purchase reference; 1985 letter on media economics; 2014 sale commentary). Buffett on Buffalo News (1977 acquisition; subsequent letters on small-newspaper economics). Buffett on Verizon (2020 commentary). Stratechery by Ben Thompson — aggregation theory and the platform-vs-pipe distinction is the canonical contemporary primer. Pat Dorsey on network-effect moats specifically. The Pew Research Journalism reports for free industry-level data on media. The FCC's annual broadband report. eMarketer / Insider Intelligence for ad-spend data where free summaries exist. Damodaran's media, telecom, and online-advertising sector data. Howard Marks's 2000 "bubble.com" memo for the canonical writeup of the dot-com era's ad-cycle excess. Substack and contemporary writers — Matthew Ball's writing on streaming and the metaverse, Andrew Marchand or other media-industry beat reporters as cited.

Length: 5,000–7,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Communication Services businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
