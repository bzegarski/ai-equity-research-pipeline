## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the CONSUMER DISCRETIONARY sector to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this sector?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. I read company writeups across every GICS sector, and I am green on most of them. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses. I want a sector primer that does the same.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for how a Consumer Discretionary business actually works — the dominant economic engine, the metrics that move the value, the customer types, the typical risks across the consumer cycle, and the historical analogs that recur. When I then read a writeup of a single Consumer Discretionary company, the primer should arm me to ask the right questions and recognize the company-specific facts in their proper sector context.

What to attend to particularly for Consumer Discretionary. (a) The defining feature of the sector is sensitivity to discretionary household spending — when consumers tighten, the wallet cuts here first. The cyclical / structural-growth divide matters most here. (b) Brand moats are the dominant moat type, and Buffett's See's Candies discussion (1972 acquisition, 1983 letter, 2007 letter) is the canonical primer on what a real consumer brand moat looks like and what it doesn't — geographic limits, the "must be continuously rebuilt = no moat" warning, the relationship between brand and pricing power. (c) Apple's transformation from a "tech" stock to a "consumer brand" in Buffett's portfolio (2016+ position) shows how the wisdom-tradition framework re-classifies businesses by their actual moat shape rather than their GICS code. (d) Restaurants, retail, and travel each have very different unit economics — restaurant unit economics (sales per location, prime-cost ratio, payback period); retail unit economics (sales per square foot, inventory turn, gross-margin-per-customer); hotel and lodging (RevPAR, occupancy, ADR). Build the primer around these unit-economic differences. (e) The role of customer-acquisition cost vs lifetime value is more visible in this sector than in most — direct-to-consumer brands lived and died on it; e-commerce platforms are organized around it.

The structure I want. Lead with the divide between defensive Discretionary (auto parts aftermarket, dollar stores) and cyclical Discretionary (luxury, autos, leisure). Use See's Candies and Apple as the two canonical brand-moat case studies — the masters have written about both, and their economics teach most of what matters. Walk the metrics that matter (no more than 6, each defined inline using a real-company number — same-store sales growth, sales per square foot or per location, gross margin / unit, customer-acquisition payback, inventory turns, free-cash-flow conversion through the cycle). Then the typical risks (consumer-credit cycle, fashion/taste shifts, e-commerce disintermediation, brand mismanagement, working-capital swings). Then recurring historical patterns (the 2008 consumer collapse, the 2020 pandemic split between durable goods boom and services bust, the 2022–24 inflation squeeze on consumer wallets). Then the books / writers / data sources to keep open (the Census Bureau's monthly retail sales report; the BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey; the National Retail Federation; sector-focused investor letters). Finally, the GICS sub-industries inside Consumer Discretionary, with one paragraph per sub-industry naming its dominant mechanic: Auto Components, Automobiles, Distributors, Diversified Consumer Services, Hotels Restaurants & Leisure, Household Durables (homebuilders), Internet & Direct Marketing Retail (Amazon and the rest), Leisure Products, Multiline Retail, Specialty Retail, Textiles Apparel & Luxury Goods.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs (Buffett on See's; Marks on 2007–08). Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose. ESG ratings imported from third-party scorers without engaging what they mean for the unit economics.

Source preferences. Buffett's letters covering See's (1972, 1983, 2007), Apple (2016+), Coca-Cola (1988+ — technically Staples but the brand-moat lesson transfers), the Burlington Industries cautionary tale (1985 letter), Berkshire's auto group (Van Tuyl 2014). Howard Marks on consumer-credit and consumer-cycle behavior. Damodaran's data on retail, restaurants, autos. Pat Dorsey's Little Book That Builds Wealth on brand moats specifically. Mauboussin's Base Rate Book on consumer-growth profiles. The National Retail Federation, the U.S. Census monthly retail report, the BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey. Contemporary thoughtful writers on consumer brands and retail (Ben Thompson at Stratechery on platform-aggregation in commerce; sector substacks).

Length: 4,500–6,500 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Consumer Discretionary businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
