## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY sector to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this sector?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. I read company writeups across every GICS sector, and I am green on most of them. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses. I want a sector primer that does the same.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for how an Information Technology business actually works — and IT is a sector where the engines really do differ enough that one primer voice has to acknowledge them separately. Pure software (license + maintenance, then SaaS subscriptions) earns the highest gross margins in any sector but spends an enormous amount on customer acquisition. Cloud infrastructure (the hyperscalers) is a capital-intensive, scale-economics business that looks more like utilities than software once the economics are stripped down. Semiconductors operate on a multi-year capex cycle with a fab-construction lead time that produces classic boom-bust supply waves. IT services is essentially professional services with a tech sticker. Each engine has different metrics, different moat shapes, and different cycle exposures.

What to attend to particularly for Information Technology. (a) Buffett's Apple position (2016 onward) is the wisdom-tradition's loudest signal that Tech is not categorically off-limits to value investors — but it is a signal about Apple-as-consumer-brand, not Apple-as-tech. Pull on the 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022, 2024 letters where Buffett describes the position; observe what he is and isn't saying. (b) Buffett's IBM position (2011 purchase, 2018 exit at a loss) is the cautionary lesson on assuming that a legacy enterprise-software incumbent has a more durable moat than secular threats deserve. (c) The SaaS metrics (ARR, NRR, magic number, Rule of 40, RPO, customer-acquisition payback) require dedicated treatment because they are the canonical first-language of public IT investing. Define each one inline using a real company's number. (d) The hyperscaler capex super-cycle (2024 onward, AI-driven) is the central present story — Microsoft's calendar-2026 capex of roughly $190B against ~$300B+ revenue is industrial-business intensity, not software intensity, and the wisdom-tradition framework needs to engage that head-on. (e) Network effects, switching costs, and intangible-asset moats live here in their purest form — Stratechery's writing on aggregation theory and the value-chain-disruption framework is the canonical secondary literature.

The structure I want. Lead with the recognition that IT is several distinct engines and frame the primer around them: pure software (SaaS), cloud infrastructure, semiconductors, IT services, hardware. For each, walk the unit economics, the metrics that matter, the moat shape, and the cycle. Use canonical companies as worked examples (Microsoft for the diversified hyperscaler-plus-software case; Salesforce or ServiceNow for pure SaaS; NVIDIA / TSMC / ASML for semiconductors at different points in the value chain; IBM / Accenture for IT services; Apple for the consumer-brand-disguised-as-tech case). Define every SaaS metric inline using a real-company number. Walk the historical patterns: 1999–2002 dot-com bust, 2008 enterprise-software resilience, 2014–18 cloud transition, 2020 pandemic acceleration, 2022 software-multiples reset, 2024–26 AI capex cycle. Engage Buffett's IBM as the cautionary lesson, and Apple as the recharacterization lesson. Then the GICS sub-industries with one paragraph each: Communications Equipment, Electronic Equipment Instruments & Components, IT Services, Semiconductors & Semiconductor Equipment, Software, Technology Hardware Storage & Peripherals.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs (Buffett on See's; Marks on 2007–08). Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose. ESG ratings imported from third-party scorers without engaging what they mean for the unit economics.

Source preferences. Buffett's letters covering Apple (2016+) and IBM (2011, 2018 exit). Howard Marks on the 2000 internet bubble (the original 2000 memo "bubble.com" is essential). Stratechery by Ben Thompson — the canonical contemporary primer on tech business models, especially aggregation theory. Bessemer Venture Partners' State of the Cloud reports for SaaS unit economics. SaaStr for SaaS metrics primers. a16z's "16 startup metrics" and "16 more startup metrics" essays. Damodaran's tech-sector data and his book Narrative and Numbers. Mauboussin's Counterpoint Global / Morgan Stanley papers on intangible assets, growth durability, and the math of value and growth in software. Stratechery's daily updates and the back catalog. Specific industry references — IDC for hardware shipments; Gartner for enterprise software; SemiAnalysis for the semiconductor stack.

Length: 5,000–7,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Information Technology businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
