## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the REAL ESTATE sector to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this sector?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. I read company writeups across every GICS sector, and I am green on most of them. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses. I want a sector primer that does the same.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for how a Real Estate business actually works — most importantly, the REIT (Real Estate Investment Trust) structure that defines most listed real estate, the AFFO and FFO metrics that replace earnings as the primary measure of cash generation, and the cap-rate thinking that's the canonical valuation language for real estate assets.

What to attend to particularly for Real Estate. (a) The REIT structural logic — to qualify as a REIT under U.S. tax law, the company must distribute at least 90% of taxable income as dividends, which means REITs are bond-proxy-flavored businesses with limited retained-earnings reinvestment capacity and high sensitivity to long-rate cycles. The investor's job is to assess whether the property portfolio's cash-flow growth and asset appreciation justify the price relative to the dividend stream. (b) FFO (Funds From Operations = net income + depreciation + amortization − gains on property sales) is the canonical earnings substitute for REITs because GAAP depreciation on real property is largely fictional in many sub-industries. AFFO (Adjusted FFO) further nets out maintenance capex and is the better cash measure. (c) Cap rate (capitalization rate = NOI / property value) is the canonical valuation language — the inverse of a P/E for property. The spread between cap rates and 10-year Treasury yields is the central macro variable for the sector. (d) The wisdom-tradition writers have written less about REITs specifically than about most sectors — Buffett owns HomeServices of America (residential brokerage, technically not a REIT) but rarely writes about listed REITs; Sam Zell's Equity Office and Equity Residential are the canonical opportunistic-real-estate-investor case studies but Zell is not in the standard reading list. Pat Dorsey's chapter on real estate is short. Howard Marks's commentary on real estate cycles in his memos is the most directly useful wisdom-tradition input. Damodaran has written extensively on real estate; pull on him heavily. (e) Sub-industry economics differ wildly — apartment REITs vs office REITs vs industrial / logistics REITs vs data-center REITs (Equinix, Digital Realty — sometimes thought of as Tech but structurally REITs) vs healthcare REITs vs storage REITs (Public Storage) vs hotel REITs vs retail REITs. Each has a different cycle exposure and a different moat shape.

The structure I want. Open with the REIT structure and the FFO / AFFO / cap-rate vocabulary — these are the load-bearing concepts that the rest of the primer rests on. Define each inline using a real-company number from a representative REIT. Walk the metrics that matter (no more than 6, each defined inline — same-store NOI growth, occupancy rate, lease expiration ladder, debt / EBITDA or net-debt / annualized-NOI, AFFO payout ratio, implied cap rate vs market cap rate). Then the typical risks (long-rate cycles for the bond-proxy sub-industries, supply waves arriving at the wrong time, tenant-credit cycles for office and retail, structural shifts in workspace demand for office REITs specifically, e-commerce shift impact on retail REITs and on industrial / logistics REITs in opposite directions). Then recurring historical patterns (the 1990 commercial-real-estate bust, the 2007–09 office and retail collapse, the 2020 pandemic split between booming industrial / logistics and crashing hotels and retail, the 2022 rate-spike re-rating). Then the GICS sub-industries with one paragraph each: Real Estate Management & Development, Equity Real Estate Investment Trusts (the major property types — Diversified, Health Care, Hotel & Resort, Industrial, Office, Residential, Retail, Specialized including Data Center and Self-Storage).

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs (Buffett on See's; Marks on 2007–08). Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose. ESG ratings imported from third-party scorers without engaging what they mean for the unit economics.

Source preferences. NAREIT (reit.com) — the canonical REIT-industry primer source; the REIT 101 series teaches FFO, AFFO, and cap rate from scratch. CCIM Institute on commercial real estate fundamentals. Howard Marks's Oaktree memos engaging real-estate cycles (especially the 2007–08 series and the 2022 rate-spike commentary). Damodaran's real-estate sector data and his book on valuation as it covers real estate. Pat Dorsey's Little Book chapter on real estate moats (regional monopoly in some sub-industries; otherwise rare). The Federal Reserve's Senior Loan Officer Survey for commercial real estate underwriting standards. Sam Zell's autobiography Am I Being Too Subtle? for the canonical opportunistic-real-estate-investor voice. The major REITs' own investor-day decks — Public Storage, Prologis, Equinix, AvalonBay — as worked-example primer material.

Length: 4,500–6,500 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Real Estate businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
