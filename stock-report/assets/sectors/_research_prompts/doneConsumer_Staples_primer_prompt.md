## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the CONSUMER STAPLES sector to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this sector?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. I read company writeups across every GICS sector, and I am green on most of them. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses. I want a sector primer that does the same.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for how a Consumer Staples business actually works — the dominant economic engine (typically: brand-driven pricing power against essentially flat unit volume), the metrics that move the value, the customer types, the typical risks, and the historical analogs that recur.

What to attend to particularly for Consumer Staples. (a) Coca-Cola is THE wisdom-tradition primer for this sector — Buffett's 1988 purchase, the 1993 letter where he calls the business "inevitable," the multi-decade compounding of dividends and intrinsic value through 2025. The Coca-Cola economics (concentrate-and-syrup business model, distribution-bottler relationship, brand premium converted to pricing power, low capital intensity) teach most of what matters about Staples. (b) See's Candies sits in Consumer Discretionary by GICS but its lessons about brand moats, regional limits, and pricing-power-as-engine are directly transferrable — explicitly bridge the two. (c) The big secular pressure of the 2010s–2020s has been private-label growth (store brands like Costco's Kirkland Signature, Trader Joe's, Aldi own-label) eroding the brand premium for marginal-utility products. The primer should teach which Staples categories resist private-label (premium spirits, infant formula, prescription-grade OTC) and which don't (commodity packaged food, paper goods, cleaning supplies). (d) Buffett's Kraft Heinz episode (2015 deal, 2019 write-down, public admission that Buffett "overpaid") is the load-bearing cautionary lesson on brand decay and the limits of cost-cutting as a strategy. (e) Costco straddles Staples (Food & Staples Retailing GICS sub-industry) and is one of Charlie Munger's longest-held positions; the membership-model unit economics it pioneered are sector-defining and worth dedicated treatment.

The structure I want. Lead with what makes Staples distinctive (brand-driven pricing power, low unit-volume growth, low capital intensity, defensive in recessions). Use Coca-Cola, See's (cross-sector but apt), Costco, and one defensive household-products company (P&G, Colgate) as the worked examples. Walk the metrics that matter (no more than 6, each defined inline — organic volume vs price growth, gross margin and gross-margin defense across input-cost cycles, marketing-to-sales ratio, return on tangible capital, free-cash-flow margin, dividend / buyback yield). Then the typical risks (private-label substitution, generation-shift in consumption habits, input-cost inflation outpacing pricing power, distribution disintermediation by direct-to-consumer brands, regulatory / tax pressure on tobacco and alcohol). Then recurring historical patterns (the 2008 defensive outperformance, the 2014–17 consumer-staples bond-proxy bubble and reversal, the 2021–23 input-cost squeeze and pricing-power test). Then the GICS sub-industries inside Consumer Staples, one paragraph each: Beverages (alcoholic and non-alcoholic — different economics), Food Products, Food & Staples Retailing (the Costco / Walmart / Kroger family with their power-buyer-vs-supplier dynamic), Household Products, Personal Products, Tobacco.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs (Buffett on See's; Marks on 2007–08). Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose. ESG ratings imported from third-party scorers without engaging what they mean for the unit economics.

Source preferences. Buffett's Coca-Cola letters (1988, 1989, 1993, and onward — cite each year). Buffett's See's writing (1972 acquisition reference, 1983 letter on brand pricing power, 2007 letter as the canonical economic-goodwill case). Buffett's Kraft Heinz cautionary commentary (2019 write-down letter and subsequent annual remarks). Howard Marks on consumer cycles and on the 2014–17 staples-as-bond-proxy episode. Damodaran's data on beverages, food, household products. Pat Dorsey's Little Book That Builds Wealth on brand and intangible moats. The IRI / Circana / NielsenIQ market-share and price/volume data where free public summaries exist. Substack and blog writers thoughtful on Staples (the Berkshire-following ecosystem, sector-focused value blogs).

Length: 4,000–6,000 words. Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Consumer Staples businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
