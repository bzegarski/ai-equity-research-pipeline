## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the INDUSTRIALS sector to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this sector?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. I read company writeups across every GICS sector, and I am green on most of them. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses. I want a sector primer that does the same.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for how an Industrials business actually works — the dominant economic engine, the metrics that move the value, the customer types, the typical risks, and the historical analogs that recur. When I then read a writeup of a single Industrials company, the primer should arm me to ask the right questions and recognize the company-specific facts in their proper sector context.

What to attend to particularly for Industrials. This is the most heterogeneous of the eleven GICS sectors and a single primer voice has to acknowledge that openly. Five distinct economic engines need separate treatment.

(a) Railroads and transportation infrastructure — long-cycle moats from physical track, terminals, port access, and right-of-way; return-on-tangible-capital businesses; Buffett's BNSF acquisition (2009) and the related letters are the canonical wisdom-tradition writing here.

(b) Aerospace & Defense — oligopolistic structure (Boeing/Airbus duopoly in commercial; Lockheed/Raytheon/Northrop/General Dynamics in defense); enormous switching costs in tier-1 supply (Precision Castparts is the Buffett 2015 acquisition example); long government-contract cycles; reputational risk that compounds (737 MAX).

(c) Industrial conglomerates and machinery — Honeywell, Emerson, Caterpillar, Deere, Illinois Tool Works (ITW). Capex-cyclical; aftermarket service revenue is the durable layer; quality of capital allocation across the cycle is the management question. Marmon (Berkshire 2008 letter) and Buffett's broader thinking on niche-leader industrial businesses applies.

(d) Airlines, road & rail, marine — the canonical Buffett "bad business model" example (commodity-priced output, very high fixed costs, capacity additions arriving at the wrong time). The 2016–2020 Berkshire airlines position and 2020 exit is the worked story; the BNSF contrast is the lesson about which transportation businesses earn excess returns.

(e) Commercial & Professional Services — staffing (Robert Half, ManpowerGroup), payroll outsourcing (Paychex, ADP), credit-bureau and information services (Equifax, Experian, Fair Isaac / FICO), facility services. Float economics, regulatory complexity-as-a-feature, distribution-channel moats (CPA referrals, broker networks). The 1996 Buffett letter on GEICO's distribution-cost moat is structurally similar; the float-on-payroll-tax-dollars dynamic at PAYX/ADP is its own thing and should be taught explicitly.

The structure I want. Open with the recognition that "Industrials" is a label covering five different kinds of business and frame the primer around them. For each engine, walk through what it means to make a dollar, the metrics that matter, the moat shape that's possible (and why it's rare in some sub-industries), and the historical analogs. Use a small number of canonical companies the wisdom-tradition writers have actually written about (BNSF, Marmon, Precision Castparts, FlightSafety from the Berkshire side; Buffett's airlines history; the comparison-to-GEICO for distribution-moat-services businesses). Then the GICS sub-industries inside Industrials, with one paragraph per sub-industry naming its dominant mechanic: Aerospace & Defense, Building Products, Construction & Engineering, Electrical Equipment, Industrial Conglomerates, Machinery, Trading Companies & Distributors, Commercial Services & Supplies, Professional Services (the Paychex/ADP family), Air Freight & Logistics, Airlines, Marine, Road & Rail, Transportation Infrastructure.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs (Buffett on See's; Marks on 2007–08). Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose. ESG ratings imported from third-party scorers without engaging what they mean for the unit economics.

Source preferences. Buffett's letters where Industrials businesses appear (BNSF 2009, Marmon 2008, Precision Castparts 2015, FlightSafety 1996, the airlines years 2016–2020; cite by year). The Association of American Railroads. The American Trucking Associations. The Aerospace Industries Association. SIA / SOCMA / API as relevant for sub-industry. Damodaran's industry data for transportation, machinery, professional services. Pat Dorsey on moat shapes inside diversified industrials. Mauboussin's Base Rate Book for capex-cyclical growth profiles. Contemporary thoughtful writers on Industrials sub-segments — sector substacks, rail-focused / aerospace-focused investor blogs.

Length: 5,000–7,000 words (this sector earns extra length because of its heterogeneity). Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Industrials businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
