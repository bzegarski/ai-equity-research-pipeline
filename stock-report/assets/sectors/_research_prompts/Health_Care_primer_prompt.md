## The prompt (copy-paste into Claude.ai's Research mode)

```
What does an individual long-term investor in the Buffett / Graham / Fisher / Munger / Marks tradition need to know about the HEALTH CARE sector to read a single-company writeup intelligently and to ask the right questions when studying any business in this sector?

Who I am. A long-term individual investor, not a professional analyst. I read company writeups across every GICS sector, and I am green on most of them. The reading list shaping the writing I want is The Essays of Warren Buffett (Cunningham), Poor Charlie's Almanack, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (Fisher), The Warren Buffett Way (Hagstrom), The Investment Checklist (Shearn), Security Analysis (Graham & Dodd), The Most Important Thing (Marks). These books teach by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses. I want a sector primer that does the same.

What I want from a primer. After reading it, I should have intuition for how a Health Care business actually works — but Health Care is more genuinely several different industries than most GICS sectors, so the primer needs to teach each engine separately. The reader should leave knowing the pharma R&D-and-patent-cliff economics, the medical-device switching-cost economics, the managed-care regulated-spread economics, the biotech option-heavy economics, and how the FDA cycle shapes all of them.

What to attend to particularly for Health Care. (a) Pharmaceuticals operate on a research-and-patent business model — long fixed development costs (10+ year, billion-dollar trials), winner-take-most rewards inside a category, then a patent cliff that takes 80–90% of revenue away in 12 months. The pharma question is always "what's in the pipeline against what comes off patent." (b) Medical devices have switching-cost moats at the doctor / institution level — Medtronic, Stryker, Boston Scientific own surgeon retraining costs that operate exactly like the IT director's switching cost in software. (c) Managed care (UnitedHealth, Elevance, Humana, Cigna, CVS Aetna) operates on a regulated medical-loss-ratio spread plus a vertical-integration premium where it works (UnitedHealth's Optum is the canonical case). (d) Biotech is structurally an option-portfolio business — most pipelines fail; a few succeed enormously; valuation is more like venture than like equity. Munger's repeated skepticism of the average biotech investor's ability to handicap clinical-trial outcomes is load-bearing. (e) Life-sciences tools (Thermo Fisher, Danaher, Agilent) and contract-research organizations sit alongside as the "picks and shovels" plays — selling to all of pharma without any single drug bet. (f) The wisdom-tradition writers have written less about Health Care than other sectors — Buffett's brief J&J position and his recent Sanofi/Pfizer trades are the closest direct input. Pull on Damodaran's pharma writing (long-running) and on Mauboussin's intangible-investment work, both of which engage Health Care economics seriously. Charlie Munger on the difficulty of biotech and on managed-care incentive structures (Daily Journal annual meetings) is the wisdom-tradition voice closest to dispositive.

The structure I want. Lead with the recognition that Health Care is several different sectors under one GICS label and frame the primer around the five engines above. For each engine, walk the unit economics, the metrics that matter, the moat shape that's possible, and the typical risk pattern. Use a small number of canonical companies as worked examples (Pfizer or Merck for pharma; Medtronic or Stryker for med devices; UnitedHealth for managed care; Vertex for biotech franchise; Thermo Fisher for life sciences tools). Then the typical risks (FDA approval risk, patent cliffs, payer pushback on drug pricing, regulatory pressure on PBM and managed-care vertical integration, biotech clinical-trial failure rates). Then recurring historical patterns (the 1990s Hillarycare scare, the 2003 Medicare Part D rollout and pharma re-rating, the 2014 GILD hepatitis-C franchise, the 2018 PCSK9-class disappointment, the 2020 vaccine rush). Then the GICS sub-industries with one paragraph each: Health Care Equipment & Supplies, Health Care Providers & Services, Health Care Technology, Biotechnology, Pharmaceuticals, Life Sciences Tools & Services.

Style I love. Concrete subjects acting on concrete objects. One specific testable example per abstract claim. Well-chosen historical analogs (Buffett on See's; Marks on 2007–08). Plain English ahead of jargon, jargon defined inline on first use. Sentence rhythm. The small earned surprise.

Style I reject. Institutional sell-side templates (target prices, CAPM, beta-driven discount rates). Generic macro framing. Formulaic SWOT and pasted Porter five-forces. Numbers without narrative. Throat-clearing prose. ESG ratings imported from third-party scorers without engaging what they mean for the unit economics.

Source preferences. The FDA Orange Book (free, the canonical patent-and-exclusivity database — most useful primer reference for understanding patent cliffs). PhRMA fact sheets on industry economics. BIO (Biotechnology Innovation Organization) educational resources. The CMS National Health Expenditure data. Buffett's brief Health Care commentary across Berkshire letters; Munger's Daily Journal remarks where Health Care appears. Damodaran's pharmaceutical sector data (pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar). Mauboussin's writing on intangible investment and the option-pricing nature of pipeline-dependent businesses. Pat Dorsey on Health Care moats specifically (med-device switching costs, pharma patents). The Wall Street Journal Health Care coverage; STAT News for biotech-specific reporting; Endpoints News.

Length: 5,500–7,500 words (this sector earns extra length because the engines are more different from each other than the sub-industry list suggests). Organize however best serves the answer.

Note: this is research about how to think about Health Care businesses in general. Don't analyze any specific company as an investment.
```
