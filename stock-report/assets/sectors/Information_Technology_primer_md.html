How to Read an IT Sector Writeup: A Primer for the Long-Term Investor

The Information Technology sector is the one place in the GICS taxonomy where a generalist's instinct to look for a single "engine" actively misleads. Five very different machines wear the same uniform. A pure software company prints near-zero-marginal-cost revenue against a customer who has buried the product in their workflow. A hyperscaler now spends like a railroad. A semiconductor company lives on a four-year capex cycle that no executive can fully control. An IT services firm rents out human beings by the hour with a thin layer of intellectual property on top. A hardware company sells a depreciating physical object — except when the physical object is an iPhone, in which case it is a consumer brand pretending to be hardware.

This primer walks each engine in turn — unit economics, the metrics that matter, the shape of the moat, the shape of the cycle — and uses real companies and real numbers to keep the abstractions honest. It interleaves the historical episodes that shaped contemporary practice (the dot-com bust, 2008, the cloud transition, the pandemic acceleration, the 2022 reset, the AI capex super-cycle now under way) and the two Buffett positions, IBM and Apple, that frame how the wisdom tradition has tried to engage with technology. It treats Howard Marks's January 2000 bubble.com memo as the canonical contemporaneous voice on this question, and Ben Thompson's aggregation theory as the canonical contemporary secondary literature.

The reader's question is operational, not academic: when you pick up a writeup on a tech business, what should you be asking?

I. The Wisdom Tradition's Quarrel With Technology

Buffett, in the 1996 letter, said the part out loud: "We are searching for operations that we believe are virtually certain to possess enormous competitive strength ten or twenty years from now. A fast-changing industry environment may offer the chance for huge wins, but it precludes the certainty we seek." That sentence, more than any other, explains why Berkshire avoided technology for forty years. It was not a hostile prejudice; it was the application of a single, very specific test — durability — to a sector where durability is the rare exception.

Two later positions tested the rule. The first, IBM, broke. The second, Apple, became the largest equity holding in Berkshire's history. Together they sketch the perimeter inside which a Graham/Fisher/Munger investor can operate in this sector, and the line outside which the engines move too fast to underwrite.

Buffett on IBM (2011 → 2018): the cautionary lesson

Berkshire revealed the IBM stake in November 2011. Buffett, on CNBC, told the now-famous anecdote about the tab-card business he had been part-owner of in the 1950s: every prospect they called on said no, "nobody's ever gotten fired by buying from IBM." That one line tells you what he thought he was buying — switching costs, an enterprise customer's congenital reluctance to rip out a working system, financial discipline ("I can think of no major company that has had better financial management"), and a CEO (Sam Palmisano) who had walked the stated 2010 Roadmap into the actual 2010 results. He bought 64 million shares at an average of about $170, eventually building to roughly 81 million shares at around $13.8 billion of cost. He explicitly said in the 2011 letter that he hoped the share price would languish — because IBM's commitment to spending roughly $50 billion on buybacks would let Berkshire's percentage ownership compound faster.

What he saw was real: IBM did have switching costs, did generate cash, did buy back stock. What he missed was that the customer's reluctance to rip out a working system stops being a moat the moment a new system appears that doesn't have to be ripped out — it can simply absorb the workload at the next refresh. That is exactly what AWS, then Azure, then Google Cloud did to IBM's services-and-mainframe stack between 2011 and 2017. The 2010 Roadmap had assumed that IBM could harvest a maturing on-premises franchise while pivoting to "Strategic Imperatives." The strategic imperatives grew; the legacy core shrank faster. By May 2017, on CNBC, Buffett said "I don't value IBM the same way that I did 6 years ago when I started buying. I've revalued it somewhat downward… IBM is a big strong company, but they've got big strong competitors too." Berkshire sold roughly a third of the position that quarter, then in early 2018 disposed of 94.5% of what remained, exiting a stake that had cost about $14.1 billion at average prices in the $150s. Adding back the $29 per share of dividends collected, the position roughly broke even on a multi-year basis. In Berkshire's compounding context, breaking even is the loss.

The cautionary lesson is precise. The error was not that Buffett invested in technology. The error was assuming that a legacy enterprise-software-and-services incumbent with sticky customers was insulated against a secular threat. Switching costs slow churn; they do not stop a customer from sending the next workload somewhere else. When you read a writeup on a legacy enterprise franchise, that is the question to ask: not "will customers leave?" but "where does new work go?"

Buffett on Apple (2016 → present): the tradition's loudest tech signal

Berkshire began buying Apple in Q1 2016. The trade was originally Todd Combs's. Buffett described the position over the next several letters in language he normally reserves for See's Candies and Coca-Cola — "extraordinary consumer franchise," "people are psychologically attached," "my third business," "Tim's managerial touch." The 2022 letter elevated Apple to one of the "Four Giants" driving Berkshire's value, second only to the insurance cluster. By the peak in late 2023 the stake was worth more than $170 billion, roughly 40% of the equity portfolio.

What Buffett was saying mattered: Apple is a consumer brand whose product has become indispensable, whose customers don't switch even when nominally cheaper alternatives are one purchase away, whose CEO is allocating capital with discipline, and whose buyback program lets long-term holders own more of the same earnings stream every year without lifting a finger. He praised Apple's services revenue (a 73.9% gross margin business that crossed $96 billion in fiscal 2024) and the ecosystem effect that funnels iPhone owners into iCloud, the App Store, Apple Pay, and Apple Music. What he was not saying mattered just as much: he never expressed a view on the iPhone hardware roadmap, on the silicon, on whether the next generation would have a better camera. He was buying the loyalty of the customer, not the technical merits of the device. As one observer put it, Buffett was treating Apple as the world's largest brand monopoly, not as a technology bet.

In 2024 Berkshire sold roughly two-thirds of the Apple position. At the May 2024 annual meeting Buffett offered a tax-rate explanation — better to pay 21% now than something higher later — that satisfied almost no one. By his 2025 commentary he had effectively conceded that he had sold too soon; the stock continued to compound. The lesson is not that he was wrong to sell. The lesson is that the entry case never depended on being right about technology, and the exit case never required being wrong about it. The position was a consumer-brand position from beginning to end. That is why it fits inside the tradition. It is also why "Buffett is in tech now" was always a misread of what was happening.

Read every IT writeup with this distinction live in your head: is the author claiming brand-and-customer-lock durability (which the tradition can underwrite) or technological-leadership durability (which the tradition has historically refused to)? Apple is the rare case where the second is true because the first is true. Most are not.

II. Engine One — Pure Software / SaaS

Unit economics

The fundamental shape of a software business is fixed cost up front, near-zero marginal cost thereafter. Writing the code is a sunk investment, often a large one; serving the millionth customer costs a hosting and support marginal expense that is small relative to price. This is why a mature subscription software company like ServiceNow runs subscription gross margins above 80%, why CrowdStrike reports non-GAAP subscription gross margin of 80% even at scale, and why Salesforce, with $37.9 billion in fiscal 2025 revenue, can throw off $12.4 billion of free cash flow. Compare with hardware (35–40% gross margin in the best case) or IT services (32% at Accenture in fiscal 2024) and the operating leverage of mature software is in a different class.

The historical evolution matters. Before roughly 2008, enterprise software was sold as a perpetual license (a one-time fee, recognized largely up front) bundled with annual maintenance (typically 18–22% of license, rebilled forever). The economics of Oracle and SAP were built on this model, and during the global financial crisis those maintenance streams proved surprisingly resilient: SAP grew software-and-services revenue 20% in constant currency in 2008 even as the world collapsed, because customers cannot easily turn off ERP. Oracle, with $10 billion of cash and 20–30 year support contracts at $180,000 a year on a typical $1 million license, weathered the storm with the kind of annuity revenue that CFOs dream about. That resilience is one of the reasons enterprise software is now considered a "defensive" growth category, even though it had been a notoriously cyclical category in the prior decade.

The subscription model that displaced licensing converts the same revenue into a smoother stream. A customer pays, say, $100,000 per year instead of $400,000 up front and $80,000 in maintenance. The 5-year revenue is similar; the timing, accounting, and risk profile are different. Under subscription, the vendor recognizes revenue ratably over the term, while billing in advance creates a deferred revenue liability on the balance sheet. As that liability runs off into the income statement and the cash that funded it sits in the bank, you can see two things at once: how much business has been signed but not yet recognized, and how much actual cash the customer paid before service began.

The metrics that matter, defined inline with real numbers

ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue): The annualized value of active subscription contracts at a point in time. ServiceNow ended its most recent reporting period at roughly $12.5 billion in ARR. CrowdStrike ended fiscal 2025 (January 31, 2025) at $4.24 billion of ARR, with $224 million of net new ARR added in Q4 alone — net new ARR being the cleanest signal of fresh demand, separating new bookings from rate increases on the installed base.

NRR / NDR (Net Revenue Retention / Net Dollar Retention): Take the cohort of customers you had a year ago. Look at what they spend with you today, including upsells, cross-sells, price increases, and net of churn and downgrades. Divide today by year-ago. Snowflake reported 126% NRR as of January 31, 2025 (and 125% as of October 31, 2025). What that means in plain English: the customer who spent $100 with Snowflake last year is now spending $125, on average, even before counting any new logo Snowflake won. This is the most powerful single indicator of a software franchise's health. McKinsey's analysis shows public B2B SaaS companies above 120% NRR trade at a median EV/revenue multiple of 21x versus 9x for those below — because NRR above 100% means the business compounds in place even if it stops selling to anyone new.

Magic Number: Annualize the change in subscription revenue from the prior quarter and divide by the prior quarter's sales-and-marketing expense. CrowdStrike disclosed this metric directly. A magic number above 1.0 means a dollar of S&M is buying more than a dollar of incremental annualized revenue — accelerate. Between roughly 0.5 and 1.0 you are still investing efficiently. Below 0.5, you are pushing on a string. The 2025 cohort median across SaaS sat at roughly 0.9, meaningfully below the 1.5+ that defined the 2018–2021 boom.

Rule of 40: Revenue growth rate plus operating margin (or free cash flow margin). The "soft" floor that says a quality SaaS business can either grow fast at zero margin or grow slowly at high margin, but must do enough of one to compensate for the other. CrowdStrike publishes a "free cash flow Rule of 40" explicitly — combining revenue growth and FCF margin. Salesforce in early fiscal 2026 announced a "50 by FY30" framework that targets the sum of constant-currency subscription growth and non-GAAP operating margin reaching 50 by 2030; the existence of that framework tells you Salesforce intends to substitute margin for the growth deceleration it sees coming.

RPO (Remaining Performance Obligations): Contracted revenue not yet recognized. The company has signed it; the customer is on the hook; it has not yet flowed through the income statement. Salesforce reported total RPO of $59.5 billion at the end of Q3 FY26 (with $29.4 billion as "current" RPO — expected within twelve months). Snowflake's RPO grew 37% year over year to $7.88 billion in late 2025. Microsoft's commercial RPO was $627 billion in its third fiscal quarter of 2026, more than double what it had been a year earlier. RPO is the closest thing software has to an order book. When RPO grows materially faster than revenue, expect future revenue acceleration. When RPO growth slows below revenue growth, the opposite.

CAC payback: How many months of gross-margin-adjusted revenue from a new customer it takes to repay the sales and marketing cost of acquiring them. Bessemer benchmarks this at under 12 months for SMB-focused businesses, 12–18 months for mid-market, under 24 months for enterprise. The 2024 industry median had drifted to roughly 18 months versus 12–14 months historically — a tell that customer acquisition has gotten harder and that competition for the marginal new logo is more intense. Read this metric alongside NRR; a long CAC payback is acceptable if the customer compounds inside the platform after that point.

Gross margin: Software gross margin is structurally different from hardware gross margin because the cost of goods sold is mostly hosting (or, historically, almost nothing), not a bill of materials. CrowdStrike's 80% non-GAAP subscription gross margin reflects a mature SaaS cost structure. Snowflake, which runs on top of AWS/Azure/GCP, sits lower at roughly 66% gross margin in fiscal 2025 because it is paying its hyperscaler suppliers a meaningful share of revenue. The takeaway: a SaaS company that depends on someone else's infrastructure has a gross-margin ceiling set by that supplier relationship. Compare this to Apple, where total gross margin of about 47% is a blend of 37% on hardware and 74% on services — a useful reminder that "gross margin" only means something once you know which engine inside the company produced it.

Operating margin at scale: The end-state question. The mature pure-SaaS template implies 30–40% operating margins at maturity once growth slows from 40%+ to 10–15%. ServiceNow operates near this template, with non-GAAP operating margins in the mid-20s and rising. Salesforce reported a non-GAAP operating margin of 35.5% in its Q3 FY26 — the model working as advertised, after a brutal 2022–2023 in which the company was forced to demonstrate it could in fact grow margin once it stopped throwing capital at growth.

Moat shape

Pure-SaaS moats are some combination of three things: switching costs (rip-and-replace pain), network effects (the product gets better the more users join), and intangible-asset moats (data accumulated, brand trust, complementary product breadth). Workday's 95%+ gross retention reflects the first; CrowdStrike's data network — every detection improves the model that protects every other customer — reflects the second; Salesforce's eighteen-product platform, where Sales Cloud customers buy Marketing Cloud, then Data Cloud, then Slack, reflects the third. The cleanest test of moat quality is NRR above 120% with low logo churn: the customer is not just staying; they are choosing to give you more wallet, voluntarily, every year.

Cycle

SaaS does not have a clean cycle of its own; it inherits two. The first is the IT-budget cycle (digestible: customers grow IT spend in good times and trim seat counts in bad times, but rarely tear out core systems). The second is the interest-rate-and-multiple cycle, which has whipped this category around violently and is the single most important context for understanding the 2021–2023 episode discussed in Section V.

III. Engine Two — Cloud Infrastructure / Hyperscalers

Unit economics, the way they used to look

Through about 2023, the hyperscaler business had the cleanest unit economics in the public-company universe. AWS at scale ran at 30–38% segment operating margin (Q3 2024 was 38.1%; 2024 full year was 37%). Microsoft Cloud, the broader bundle, reported gross margins around 70% as recently as fiscal 2024. Google Cloud crossed into operating profitability in 2023. Each of those businesses was, in shape, a utility — fixed-cost data center build, variable demand metered as compute, storage, and networking — combined with a software toolchain (databases, queues, ML services) that bolted onto the metered infrastructure and earned higher margins.

The conventional way to think about this engine was: utility-like demand stability, software-like unit economics, scale advantages so large that the world would consolidate to a handful of providers. That picture is still partly true. It is also no longer the dominant fact about the business.

The central present story: the AI capex super-cycle

In calendar 2026 the four largest US hyperscalers — Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta — are guiding to roughly $700 billion of combined capital expenditure, with Oracle adding another $50 billion and the Chinese clouds another $80 billion or so. The headline is Microsoft: $190 billion of capex against revenue expected in the $300–320 billion range. About $25 billion of the $190 billion, the company has said, is component-cost inflation (memory and chips). Alphabet has guided $180–190 billion. Amazon, $200 billion. Meta, $125–145 billion. Combined hyperscaler capex roughly doubled from 2024 to 2025 and is set to roughly double again by 2026.

To put this in historical perspective: hyperscaler capex as a share of revenue has moved from the 10–15% range of the 2010s to 45–57% in 2026 estimates. That is not software intensity. It is industrial business intensity. ExxonMobil and Chevron spend 8–10% of revenue on capex. Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern spend 18–20%. The hyperscalers in 2026 are spending more, in dollar terms, than the entire US oil-and-gas major capex budget, and as a share of revenue more than freight rail.

What this means for unit economics, return on invested capital, and depreciation:

  1. Depreciation is going to compress operating margin. A GPU and the data center it sits in cost real money and depreciate over four to six years (and there is active debate about whether the four-year assumption is generous; Microsoft has already extended useful-life assumptions multiple times). Two-thirds of Microsoft's recent quarterly capex went to short-lived assets. That implies a depreciation wave hitting the income statement starting in 2025 and accelerating. AWS's cycle of operating-margin expansion (29.6% in Q4 2023 → 36.9% in Q4 2024 → 39.5% peak in Q1 2025) had already begun rolling over by Q2 2025 to 32.9%, with management citing AI infrastructure costs.

  2. Free cash flow is taking a hit. Bank of America estimated in early 2026 that Amazon's free cash flow would be negative $28 billion in 2026; Morgan Stanley pegged it at negative $17 billion. Barclays expected Microsoft FCF to fall 28% in calendar 2026. In rough numbers, of every dollar of hyperscaler revenue earned today, somewhere between 40 and 60 cents is being plowed back into compute capacity. That ratio has no precedent in the modern history of these companies.

  3. Return on invested capital is now an open question. The bull case is that AI inference revenue scales fast enough to absorb the capex, that depreciation schedules are conservative, that pricing power on AI tokens holds, and that productivity gains in customer workflows justify continued spend. The bear case is the one Howard Marks raised in his 2025 Is It a Bubble? memo and that SemiAnalysis has documented in detail: vendor financing is proliferating, hyperscalers are leveraging balance sheets to maintain capex velocity even as revenue momentum lags, and "the boom may yet prove productive, but only if revenue catches up before credit tightens." The Microsoft commercial RPO of $627 billion suggests demand is real; the open question is at what gross margin the demand converts.

How the wisdom tradition should think about this

Three observations.

First, the category of business has shifted while the GICS code has not. A reader who has been told for fifteen years that hyperscalers are "asset-light software-economics" companies is now reading writeups about a business that, on a marginal-dollar-of-revenue basis, looks like a utility plus a chip merchant plus a real-estate developer. The wisdom tradition has a model for this — Buffett owns BNSF and runs an electric utility — but that model expects single-digit ROEs and slow growth, not 25% topline. Whether AI infrastructure will produce industrial-business returns or software-business returns or something in between is the question. Anyone who tells you they know the answer with confidence is selling you something.

Second, there is a useful Marks analog. In bubble.com, Marks observed that "airplanes and automobiles had been expected to change the world and did… and almost all of the manufacturers of both are now gone. Few things have had the impact on the world that aviation did, but from its founding through 1992, the cumulative profit of the airline industry was zero." He was quoting Buffett's 1999 Fortune piece. The point is not that AI infrastructure won't change the world — it almost certainly will — but that the company that captures the consumer and the company that builds the infrastructure are usually different companies, and the second usually loses money in aggregate. In railroads, the lasting moats came after consolidation, decades after the original buildout. The same may be true here.

Third, the differentiation among hyperscalers matters more than the headline. Microsoft's capex is largely funded by Azure plus the Microsoft 365 monopoly — a moat-protected business throwing off cash. Amazon's capex is funded by AWS plus a low-margin retail business that is a worse cushion. Alphabet's capex is funded by the search advertising monopoly, which is itself under existential threat from generative AI. Meta's capex is funded by social-network advertising, which the company is betting AI will make more valuable through better ad targeting and content recommendations. The capex looks the same; the cash-flow generation behind it does not. When you read a hyperscaler writeup, the first question is which legacy cash-cow is paying for the AI buildout, and how durable that cash cow is.

Moat shape

Switching costs (data egress fees, integration with hundreds of services), scale economies (only a handful of companies can afford a $190 billion annual capex), regulatory barriers (FedRAMP, sovereign cloud), and developer ecosystem (the millions of engineers trained on AWS APIs). The depth of those moats explains why margin compression from AI capex is being treated as cyclical rather than structural. The risk is that a parallel infrastructure stack — built around AI-specific architectures or sovereign clouds — develops alongside the hyperscalers and erodes the lock-in over the next decade.

IV. Engine Three — Semiconductors

Unit economics

The semiconductor stack splits into pieces with very different economics. NVIDIA designs GPUs and sells them with software (CUDA, networking, libraries) that turns a chip into a system; in fiscal Q3 2025 it printed $35.1 billion in revenue with 75% gross margins and roughly 50% operating margins, the highest-quality income statement of any company at that scale in the world. TSMC manufactures the chips other companies design, charging $20,000+ per advanced-node wafer and earning 56% gross margins on $90 billion of 2024 revenue against $30 billion of capital expenditure. ASML makes the lithography machines TSMC uses, holds a global monopoly on extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) systems (a single machine costs €350+ million; the next-generation High-NA system runs €380 million), and earned 51% gross margins on €28.3 billion of 2024 revenue.

These are radically different businesses. NVIDIA has a software moat (CUDA) wrapped around a fabless design business, with TSMC as its critical supplier. TSMC is a manufacturing oligopolist (effectively a duopoly with Samsung, with TSMC owning the leading edge). ASML is a single-source monopolist for an essential capital good. Each one earns extraordinary margins for different reasons.

The cycle that defines the sector

Semiconductors have been a cyclical industry for seventy years. The cycle has two drivers: capacity is inelastic (a new fab takes three to five years and $20+ billion to build) while demand is elastic (consumer electronics, autos, data centers, all swing). When demand outruns supply, prices spike and existing capacity earns extraordinary returns. The earnings draw new capacity, new capacity arrives 3–5 years later, and supply overshoots demand. Prices crash. Margins collapse. The weak players exit. Capacity is cut. Eventually demand recovers and the cycle repeats.

DRAM is the cleanest illustration. The 1995–96 boom drew about 50 announced new fabs; the 1996 bust took DRAM prices down more than 60%. The 2018–19, 2022–23, and 2024–25 cycles each followed the same shape, though with consolidation (now three players in DRAM globally) the amplitude has narrowed. Fabricated Knowledge has chronicled the 1980s and 1990s cycles in detail; the lesson is that "this time is different" has been wrong roughly fifteen times in a row.

Where AI changes the picture (and where it doesn't)

The current AI boom is a demand spike of historic proportions. NVIDIA's data center revenue grew from $4.7 billion in fiscal 2023 to ~$115 billion run-rate by fiscal Q1 2026. TSMC's high-performance computing segment is now 55% of total sales, up from low-40s two years earlier. Memory pricing has tripled in some categories. The bull case says this is structural: AI demand keeps growing for a decade, and supply (particularly HBM memory and leading-edge logic) remains tight because TSMC and SK Hynix are capital-disciplined.

The bear case says it is a cycle. Hyperscalers ordering well ahead of need. ODMs double-ordering against component shortages. The very capex-as-a-share-of-revenue figures cited above — the same numbers the bulls cite as proof of structural demand — are the kind of capacity buildup that has historically preceded every previous semiconductor downturn. SemiAnalysis (Dylan Patel) has been the closest contemporary read on which is which, and even he distinguishes carefully between segments. Memory looks late-cycle; logic at the leading edge looks earlier-cycle; networking and packaging are tight; older-node analog and power chips have already been through their own correction.

Moat shape

NVIDIA's moat is software (CUDA, twenty years of accumulated library ecosystem) plus the integrated systems business. TSMC's moat is process leadership and capital scale (a 2-nanometer fab costs $30 billion, and TSMC has decades of yield-improvement know-how that no competitor can replicate without billions of dollars and years). ASML's moat is the most pristine in technology: nobody else can make an EUV machine, and the supplier ecosystem (~800 specialty suppliers in Europe and Asia) makes the moat physically constructive. None of these moats is permanent. NVIDIA's CUDA dominance is being attacked by AMD's ROCm, by hyperscaler-designed silicon (Google TPU, Amazon Trainium, Microsoft Maia), and by open-source software like Triton. TSMC faces geopolitical risk from Taiwan-China tensions. ASML faces export controls that may eventually fragment its market.

When you read a semiconductor writeup, ask: where in the cycle is this segment, what is the ratio of capex to revenue, are inventories building or burning, and what is the customer's customer doing? (NVIDIA's customers are hyperscalers; the hyperscalers' customers are enterprises adopting AI; if enterprise adoption disappoints, the chain breaks at the second link.)

V. Engine Four — IT Services

IT services is professional services with a tech sticker. Accenture is the canonical example: $64.9 billion of fiscal 2024 revenue at a 32.6% gross margin and roughly 15% operating margin, with revenues split almost evenly between consulting (one-time engagements) and managed services (multi-year operating contracts). The economics are fundamentally those of a labor business: revenue is people-hours times bill rate, gross margin is the spread between bill rate and pay rate, operating margin is what's left after sales, training, and overhead. IBM's services business, which Buffett bought into in 2011, was a slightly more software-augmented version of the same model.

Three things distinguish best-in-class IT services from mediocre ones. First, utilization — what share of consultant capacity is billable. Second, attach rate of intellectual property — how much of a typical engagement involves Accenture-developed accelerators, frameworks, or tools rather than pure body-shop labor. Third, client concentration and stickiness — multi-year managed-services contracts with embedded vendor relationships are far more valuable than one-off consulting projects.

The cycle is real but mild. IT services revenue compresses in recessions (consulting projects get cut first; managed services stay) and accelerates in technology-transition windows (Y2K, the cloud migration of the 2010s, the AI deployment now beginning). Accenture grew managed services 11% in Q1 fiscal 2025 — a clear AI-deployment signal — even as consulting growth was more modest.

The wisdom-tradition take: this engine produces stable but unspectacular returns. Operating margins above 15% reflect real expertise; nobody in this category is going to compound at NVIDIA's rate. The honest writeup describes this as a high-quality dividend-and-buyback compounder with modest growth — not as "tech." Confusing the two is one of the most common errors in sector analysis.

VI. Engine Five — Hardware (and the Apple Exception)

Most hardware businesses are commodity-prone. Dell, HP Inc., Lenovo all run gross margins in the 20–25% range and operating margins in the mid-single digits. The product depreciates the moment it leaves the warehouse. The customer's switching cost is the cost of a new keyboard. Differentiation is service, distribution, or scale — none of which produces a moat that compounds for decades. Cisco's networking equipment business is the partial exception (gross margins in the 60s, sticky enterprise relationships, certifications and training), but even Cisco's revenue has roughly quadrupled across twenty-five years while the stock has only recently regained its 2000 peak — a striking reminder that even the durable hardware franchise can deliver a lost generation of returns when bought at the wrong multiple.

Apple is the consumer-brand exception. The iPhone is hardware — gross margins about 37%, comparable to Samsung's high-end phone business. But the position the iPhone occupies in the customer's life makes it a brand business in a way that Samsung's phones are not. About 51% of Apple's fiscal 2024 net sales of $391 billion came from the iPhone. Roughly 25% — services — runs at 74% gross margins and grew 13% year-over-year. Wearables, Mac, and iPad fill in the rest. The blended gross margin is about 47% and the operating margin is in the low-30s — a hardware company's cost structure delivering software-company profitability because the customer chose to lock themselves into the ecosystem.

This is exactly the case Buffett saw in 2016, and it is why Apple is the wisdom tradition's only sustained venture into "tech." The lesson generalizes badly. There is no second Apple. Most hardware is what hardware looks like.

VII. The Historical Walk

1999–2002: the dot-com bust

Howard Marks's bubble.com, dated January 2, 2000, is the canonical contemporaneous voice. The memo opens with the South Sea Bubble of 1720 and Sir Isaac Newton's observation that "I can calculate the motions of the heavenly bodies, but not the madness of the people." Newton sold his South Sea stock early at a profit, then bought back at the high and lost £20,000 — "not even one of the world's smartest men was immune to this tangible lesson in gravity!"

Marks then walked the parallels to 1999: the willingness to invest in money-losing companies whose business models couldn't be explained, the trade volume that exceeded fair-price calculation, the IPO underwear in which Akamai had gone public at $26 in October 1999 and closed its first day at $145, the venture funds tripling in size against historical returns, the MBA students dropping out to chase dot-com riches. The most quoted line, drawn from a Buffett Fortune piece Marks cited, was the warning that the airline and automobile industries had changed the world and produced no aggregate profit: "The key to investing is not assessing how much an industry is going to affect society, or how much it will grow, but rather determining the competitive advantage of any given company and, above all, the durability of that advantage."

The S&P 500 fell 46% from March 2000 through October 2002. The NASDAQ Composite fell roughly 80%. Cisco — the "world's most valuable company" at $80 a share in March 2000 — would not regain that level for over twenty-five years, despite quintupling its revenue in the interval. Cisco illustrates the Marks-Buffett point precisely: a great company, real profits, real growth, real moat — and a lost generation of stock-price returns because the entry multiple discounted a future that arrived but did not arrive at the price the multiple had paid for.

2008: enterprise-software resilience

The surprise of 2008 was how well legacy enterprise software held up. SAP grew software-and-services revenue 20% in constant currency for the year. Oracle, with $10 billion of cash, kept its $180,000-per-year-per-million-of-license maintenance annuity flowing. Microsoft's enterprise business, Office and Windows on the PC refresh cycle, was hit harder by the recession but recovered fast. Customers do not turn off ERP, identity systems, databases, and email when the world wobbles. They turn off discretionary IT projects. This is one of the durable lessons of the financial-crisis episode and a key reason large-cap enterprise software is now considered a defensive growth category.

2014–2018: the cloud transition

Satya Nadella took over as Microsoft CEO in February 2014 with the company at a $300 billion market cap, widely regarded as a slow-moving incumbent. He pivoted the company in a way that has now become the textbook example of a successful CEO transition. He elevated Azure (then a $1 billion business in 2015) to the strategic center of the company. He made Office 365 a subscription service and let it run on iOS, which the prior regime would not have countenanced. He acquired LinkedIn in 2016 and GitHub in 2018. He cultivated a "learn-it-all" culture that retained engineering talent. By the end of the decade Azure was the second-largest cloud infrastructure provider, Microsoft Cloud was generating $33 billion+ a quarter, and the market cap had crossed $2 trillion.

The same period saw AWS pull definitively ahead of any other cloud provider, building the tooling and ecosystem moat that makes it still the leader by revenue. Google Cloud spent the decade as a credible technical service searching for a go-to-market strategy. Oracle and IBM lost the on-premises franchise to the cloud roughly as the IBM section of this primer described. The single most important investment lesson of the period was that cloud did not commoditize software the way many feared; it created a new category of infrastructure-and-platform businesses with even better economics than the licensed-software incumbents they displaced. The wrong stocks to own through this transition were the legacy on-premises pure-plays. The right stocks to own were the cloud-native software vendors and the hyperscalers themselves.

2020: pandemic acceleration

COVID pulled forward two to three years of cloud and SaaS adoption into about nine months. Zoom is the cartoon example, but the bigger, more durable story was the broad enterprise digitization push: companies that had been talking about cloud migration for years closed the deal in weeks. Salesforce, ServiceNow, Snowflake, Workday all saw growth accelerate through 2020 and into 2021. Multiples expanded to extraordinary levels — best-of-breed SaaS traded at 50x+ forward revenue at the November 2021 peak. Bessemer's cloud-companies index reached $2.7 trillion in market cap.

2022: the multiples reset

What goes up extreme can come down extreme. The interest-rate hike cycle that began in March 2022 punctured the long-duration thesis that had supported 2021 SaaS multiples. Bessemer named the episode "SaaSacre." The cloud-companies index roughly halved. Many SaaS stocks fell 60–80% from peak. Customer behavior tightened in tandem: SaaS sales cycles stretched, customers consolidated vendors, "do more with less" became the universal message at every enterprise CFO conference.

The corporate response was the layoff wave that began in late 2022 and ran through 2023. Meta announced 11,000 layoffs in November 2022 and another 10,000 in March 2023, the second wave framed in Mark Zuckerberg's now-famous "year of efficiency" memo. Salesforce, Microsoft, Amazon, Google all followed. The consolidated effect on the SaaS category was forcing every operator to demonstrate that profitability and growth were not mutually exclusive — that the Rule of 40 mattered. Salesforce's own transformation from a low-30s growth, mid-teens operating-margin business into a mid-single-digit growth, mid-30s operating-margin business is the cleanest example. The stocks recovered through 2023 and 2024 as the math worked. The category is permanently changed: Bessemer survey data now shows the "ideal" startup profile has shifted from triple-digit growth at any cost to triple-digit growth with a 1.2x burn multiple. CAC payback worsened to roughly 18–20 months in 2024 from 12–14 historically; the magic number median fell to about 0.9. Efficient growth is now the only growth that earns a premium multiple.

2024–2026: the AI capex super-cycle

The AI capex super-cycle described in Section III is the present moment. The reader should note one cross-cutting fact: the same NVIDIA, TSMC, ASML, hyperscaler set that the wisdom tradition would historically have approached with caution is precisely the set whose capex is funding everyone else's revenue. When Microsoft commits $190 billion of 2026 capex, that money flows to NVIDIA, to TSMC, to SK Hynix, to Vertiv, to electrical infrastructure, to the gas turbines being installed to power the data centers. Reading a writeup on any of these companies requires understanding that the revenue line is being driven by a small number of customers' capital plans — and those plans are being made under enormous uncertainty about whether AI inference will produce the returns that justify the spend.

Marks, in his October 2025 Is It a Bubble? memo, did not declare a bubble but did flag the 2000-telecom-bust echoes: vendor financing, special-purpose vehicles, the imagination running ahead of the cash flows. The specific phrase he quoted from Derek Thompson — "The railroads were a bubble and they transformed America. Electricity was a bubble, and it transformed America" — captures the awkward truth. Most technological booms produce real productive infrastructure and large investor losses on the path to that productive infrastructure. The investor's job is to underwrite the few businesses that will be among the survivors at scale, while remembering that being right about the technology is not the same as being right about the price.

VIII. Moats in IT in Their Purest Form

Network effects

The canonical IT network effects are user-to-user (Facebook, LinkedIn, WhatsApp), user-to-developer (iOS App Store, Windows in its day), and data network effects (the more telemetry CrowdStrike collects from all its customers, the better its models protect each one). The defining property is increasing returns to scale: a marginal new user makes the platform more valuable to every existing user, which in turn attracts more new users. Mauboussin's research, drawing on AT&T's 1908 annual report (which already articulated the network-effect dynamic for telephone subscribers), is the canonical contemporary analysis.

Switching costs

Enterprise software locks customers in through three mechanisms simultaneously: the cost of data migration, the cost of retraining users, and the integration into adjacent systems. Salesforce's CRM database holds a company's accumulated customer history; ripping it out means losing institutional memory. Oracle and SAP ERPs hold the financial books and supply chain; switching means re-implementing accounting controls. ServiceNow's IT service management platform is wired into hundreds of internal workflows. The 98–99% logo retention rates these vendors report are not coincidental; they reflect the empirical fact that even unhappy customers rarely switch. The moat is real but not infinite — it slows churn rather than stopping it, and it is strongest against direct rip-and-replace competitors and weakest against new categories of work that route around the incumbent.

Intangible-asset moats

The Mauboussin work on intangible assets — at Counterpoint Global and earlier at Credit Suisse — is the best contemporary literature on how to think about software-company moats. Two properties of intangible assets matter most for investors. First, scalability: the marginal cost of one more customer is near zero, so winners can compound much faster than tangible-asset incumbents. Second, sunkenness and obsolescence: when an intangible investment fails, it has near-zero salvage value, so losers decline faster than tangible-asset losers. The distribution of outcomes for intangible-heavy businesses has a wider standard deviation than for tangible-heavy ones — more extreme winners and more extreme failures than the historical base rate would suggest. This is the key reason why "asset-light" is partly a myth (the spending hasn't disappeared; it has just moved from the balance sheet to the income statement) and why portfolio construction in IT requires extra care about position concentration in the long-tail winners.

Aggregation theory

Ben Thompson's Aggregation Theory (the 2015 essay and its many extensions in Stratechery) is the canonical contemporary framework for thinking about moats in consumer-facing internet businesses. The argument: in pre-internet value chains, the firm that controlled distribution won, because controlling distribution gave it leverage over both suppliers and consumers. On the internet, distribution is free. So the firm that owns the customer relationship wins, because once a firm has aggregated demand, suppliers must come to it on its terms — which commoditizes the suppliers and gives the aggregator monopoly economics. Thompson's defining examples are Google in search, Facebook in social, Amazon in retail, Netflix in streaming. Each owns a direct customer relationship that cannot be disintermediated, faces zero marginal cost on each new user, and benefits from a flywheel where suppliers commoditize themselves to access the demand pool.

The framework has limits — the music-streaming example, where labels remained more concentrated than streamers and so kept margin, is the cleanest counterexample, and Lyall Taylor's critique that aggregation theory is essentially a restatement of supply-and-demand power dynamics is fair. But the framework is the right starting point for any consumer-internet writeup, and increasingly for enterprise-software businesses with platform aspirations. The question to ask: who in this value chain has aggregated demand, who is at risk of being commoditized as a supplier, and is there an integration point (data, hardware, distribution) that gives this firm sustainable differentiation against the aggregators above and below it?

Thompson's complementary value-chain disruption framework asks where the integration in the value chain happens and whether the company's profit pool sits at the integration point. A semiconductor designer that doesn't own its fab has integrated design and software (NVIDIA + CUDA) but is exposed to TSMC's pricing power on manufacturing. A device-maker that owns the OS and the hardware (Apple) has integrated device and software, which is why Apple captures most of the smartphone industry's profit pool despite a minority unit-share. When you read an IT writeup, finding the integration point is half the battle.

IX. The GICS Sub-Industries: Brief Tour

After the engines, the GICS taxonomy. Each sub-industry maps imperfectly onto the engines above; a single company often spans several. The brief paragraphs below name the canonical players and connect each sub-industry back to the unit economics that govern it.

Software (engine: pure SaaS, with legacy license/maintenance still present). Microsoft, Oracle, Salesforce, ServiceNow, Adobe, Intuit, SAP, Workday, Snowflake, Palantir, CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks. The largest and most economically attractive sub-industry by margin, with the steepest spread between best-in-class (80% subscription gross margins, 120%+ NRR) and weakest (20–30% growth bought with 110–130% of revenue spent on sales and marketing). Read the SaaS metrics in Section II as the diagnostic toolkit for any name in this category.

Semiconductors & Semiconductor Equipment (engine: semis, with significant intra-category variation). NVIDIA, AMD, Intel, Broadcom, Qualcomm, Marvell, Texas Instruments, Analog Devices, Micron in the design-and-product layer. TSMC (foundry), GlobalFoundries, UMC in manufacturing. ASML, Applied Materials, Lam Research, KLA, Tokyo Electron in equipment. The cycle is real, the capex is huge, and segment-by-segment exposure (HBM memory, leading-edge logic, mature-node analog, networking) matters more than the headline. AI demand is rewriting the demand side; capacity discipline by the three remaining DRAM makers (Samsung, SK Hynix, Micron) is rewriting the supply side. Whether the cycle has been broken or merely shifted is the central question for the next three years.

IT Services (engine: services). Accenture, Cognizant, Infosys, TCS, Wipro, Capgemini, IBM (now mostly services and software after the 2021 Kyndryl spin), DXC. Stable mid-teens operating margins, 32–35% gross margins, growth tied to enterprise IT budget cycles plus technology-transition windows. The AI deployment phase favors this category because most enterprises will need help integrating models into workflows; the AI-eats-services bear case (consultants automated by their own product) is the principal long-term risk to monitor.

Technology Hardware, Storage & Peripherals (engine: hardware, with Apple as the consumer-brand exception). Apple is roughly 80% of the sub-industry's market cap and is not representative of it. The rest — Dell, HP Inc., NetApp, Pure Storage, Western Digital, Seagate, Logitech — exhibit the commodity-prone economics described in Section VI. Storage is a partial exception, with sticky enterprise relationships and certifications that earn slightly higher margins than PCs. The reader's first move when picking up an Apple writeup should be to check whether the author is treating it as hardware or as a consumer-brand-with-services-attached. The latter is the case the wisdom tradition can underwrite.

Communications Equipment. Cisco, Arista Networks, Juniper, Motorola Solutions, F5, Ciena. Networking gear sits between hardware and software — Cisco's IOS operating system, certifications, and enterprise relationships create real switching costs, while Arista has built a comparable franchise in the high-end data-center switch market by riding the cloud build-out. The AI capex cycle is a positive demand signal for this sub-industry (Arista's growth has accelerated as hyperscalers build AI fabrics; Cisco's stock recently regained its March 2000 peak after twenty-five years). Margins are software-like (60%+ gross margins) on the back of hardware sold at premium prices because of integrated software. The cycle tracks enterprise IT budgets plus the hyperscaler capex plan — which makes 2024–26 a structural tailwind and creates obvious downside if the AI capex cycle disappoints.

Electronic Equipment, Instruments & Components. Amphenol, TE Connectivity, Keysight, Cognex, Trimble, Zebra Technologies, Corning. The most heterogeneous sub-industry in IT — connectors, test-and-measurement gear, industrial sensors, optical components, specialty glass. Most of these businesses are better thought of as industrial-economy compounders that have been parked in the IT sector for taxonomy reasons. They earn gross margins in the 30–50% range, operate on multi-year industrial cycles, and pay reasonable dividends. Read them with the unit economics of an industrial supplier, not those of a software company. The exception is Corning, whose specialty-glass franchise (Gorilla Glass for phones, optical fiber for data centers) does carry technology-driven moat characteristics.

X. Reading the Writeup: A Final Checklist

When you sit down with an IT-sector single-company writeup, the questions to ask are engine-specific.

For a pure SaaS / software company: What is the NRR, and is it stable, rising, or falling? What is the magic number? What is the CAC payback period? Where is the company on the Rule of 40? Is RPO growing faster or slower than revenue? What is the customer concentration? What is the product breadth (one-product-pony or multi-product platform)? Where is the moat — switching costs, network effects, or intangible assets — and what is the specific evidence?

For a hyperscaler: What share of revenue is being reinvested as capex? How much of capex is short-lived (GPUs, servers) versus long-lived (data centers, land)? What is the depreciation schedule? Which legacy cash-cow is funding the AI buildout, and how durable is it? What is the segment operating margin trajectory? What is the implied return on AI capex if growth normalizes?

For a semiconductor company: Where in the value chain does this company sit (design, manufacturing, equipment, memory)? What is the segment exposure (HBM, leading-edge logic, mature-node, networking)? What is the inventory situation across the customer base? Where is the cycle? What is the customer concentration? What capex commitments are already in the ground that will produce capacity in 2027–28 regardless of demand?

For an IT services company: What is the utilization rate? What is the consulting-versus-managed-services mix? What is the IP attach rate? What is the customer concentration and length of contract? How is the company positioned for the AI deployment wave specifically?

For hardware: Is this Apple, or is this everything else? If it is Apple, the question is whether the consumer-brand-and-ecosystem moat is intact, what is happening to services revenue and services gross margin, and what is Tim Cook's capital allocation doing. If it is everything else, the question is whether commodity dynamics are improving or deteriorating, and whether the company has any genuine differentiation worth paying for.

For all of them: What is the entry multiple, and what are the embedded assumptions about growth durability? Marks's 2000 memo, his 2025 memo, and Buffett's 1996 letter all reduce, in the end, to the same proposition. Predicting that a technology will change the world is not investing. Predicting which company within the technology will earn durable economic returns, and at what price you can buy that company today, is investing. The wisdom tradition has been imperfect on the second question in IT — IBM was wrong, Apple was eventually right but Buffett sold too early, and several other names (Amazon, Microsoft pre-Nadella, NVIDIA pre-2017) were never owned at all. But the framework still works. It just demands that the reader take seriously that there are five different engines under one taxonomy hood, and that asking the right question requires knowing which engine one is reading about.

The reward, when one reads carefully, is that the IT sector still contains the densest concentration of high-quality compounders in any sector of the public market. The five engines are different. The investment work is similar to what the tradition has always asked: find the durable economics, refuse to overpay, and remember that being right about the technology is a smaller problem than being right about the price.
