# The Operating Manual: How to Write Single-Company Investment Research That Earns Its Keep Across a Cycle

## Why this guide exists, and who it is for

A great writeup is not a pitch. It is an operating manual for owning a piece of a business through a decade of weather you cannot forecast. The reader you are writing for is your own future self — the slightly tired, slightly distracted version of you who, in 2031, will glance at a headline and need to decide whether to add to the position, hold, or get out. The secondary reader is the spouse at dinner who asks, in good faith, "wait, what does this company actually do?" and deserves a sentence rather than a lecture. If your writeup serves those two readers, it will outlast every quarterly earnings cycle, every analyst-day deck, every macro panic. If it does not, no amount of formatting can save it.

This is a deeply opinionated essay about what excellent single-company research looks like for a long-term, individual, Buffett-Graham-Fisher-Munger-Marks investor — and, just as importantly, what it does *not* look like. The reading list at the heart of this tradition — *The Essays of Warren Buffett* edited by Lawrence Cunningham; Munger's *Poor Charlie's Almanack*; Fisher's *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits*; Hagstrom's *The Warren Buffett Way*; Shearn's *The Investment Checklist*; Graham & Dodd's *Security Analysis*; Marks's *The Most Important Thing* — shares a single feature: it teaches by anchoring abstractions to concrete businesses, then telling stories about those businesses long enough that the abstractions stick. Buffett does not explain "economic goodwill" in the abstract; he buys See's Candies for $25 million in 1972, watches it earn $82 million pre-tax on $40 million of capital thirty-five years later, and writes about it in 1983 and again in 2007 until you cannot forget the lesson (Berkshire Hathaway 1983 and 2007 letters). Marks does not warn about cycles in the abstract; he writes "Risk" in January 2006 and "It's All a Big Mistake" in June 2012 because specific moments demanded specific words. Fisher does not ask you to "do qualitative research"; he tells you to go talk to the company's competitors, ex-employees, and customers, and calls that "scuttlebutt" (*Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits*, Chapter 2).

Your writeup should do the same thing. The contract with the future reader is simple: after one careful reading and zero re-reading, the reader can explain at dinner what the business sells, who buys it, why customers stay, where the cash goes, and what would break it. They can recall numbers because the numbers are tied to stories. They have learned the industry through the company. And — most importantly — they know what news would matter, so when the news arrives in five years, they can react instead of panic.

That is the standard. Everything that follows is in service of it.

## Part I — The Philosophy: a writeup is an operating manual, not a buy/sell pitch

The single most useful sentence in this entire tradition may be Buffett's, in his 1996 letter: "What an investor needs is the ability to correctly evaluate selected businesses. Note that word 'selected': You don't have to be an expert on every company, or even many. You only have to be able to evaluate companies within your circle of competence. The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital" (Berkshire Hathaway 1996 letter, reproduced in Cunningham's *Essays*). Read it as a directive about writing as much as about investing. The purpose of a writeup is not to demonstrate that you understand every company; it is to record, in a form your future self can audit, that you understand *this* one — and to make the boundary of that understanding visible.

That reframe has three implications.

The first is that the writeup is permanent and the position is not. Buffett's 1988 line — "when we own portions of outstanding businesses with outstanding managements, our favorite holding period is forever" (Berkshire Hathaway 1988 letter, on the Coca-Cola purchase) — is often misquoted as a slogan about never selling. It is actually a sentence about temperament: the document you produce should be written as if you intend to hold forever, because that posture forces you to understand the business at the level of a private owner rather than a renter of stock prices. If you later sell, fine. But the artifact should reflect ownership thinking.

The second is that the implicit reader is generous, not adversarial. Sell-side reports are written for a buyer who is paid to nitpick the model and, six weeks later, will not remember the company's name. Your reader is you, in five years, holding a position that has either compounded admirably or behaved badly, trying to remember why you bought it. That reader does not need a CAPM (Capital Asset Pricing Model — the academic formula that prices risk via beta) calculation to one decimal place. That reader needs to know what hypothesis the position rests on and what evidence would falsify it.

The third is that the document should be useful in proportion to its boredom. Excellent writeups are quiet. They do not have target prices. They do not have ratings. They do not have catalysts. They have *expectations* — what the price implies must be true — and they have *risks* — what would have to break for the implied future not to arrive. Howard Marks said it cleanly in *The Most Important Thing* and his 2022 memo "I Beg to Differ" (July 26, 2022): "Readily available quantitative information with regard to the present cannot be the source of superior performance." The edge is in the patient, written articulation of what you believe and why; the audit trail is what makes you a second-level thinker rather than a first-level one.

A good test of philosophy: if your writeup would embarrass you when someone asked, in plain English, *why is this section here*, the section does not belong. CAPM-derived discount rates fail this test. Pasted Porter five-forces frameworks fail this test. ESG (environmental, social, governance) score tables, attached without engaging the question of what those scores mean for the *business*, fail this test. Each of them is throat-clearing for an audience you do not have.

## Part II — Principles that distinguish the individual-investor writeup from the sell-side report

Five principles flow from this philosophy. They are opinionated and they take sides.

**Principle 1: Voice is concrete and owner-flavored.** Sell-side prose is in a passive voice spoken to no one in particular: "the company is well-positioned to capitalize on secular tailwinds in the verticalized SaaS landscape." Your writeup uses concrete subjects and concrete objects, like Buffett describing GEICO in his 1996 letter: "The company's competitive strength flows directly from its position as a low-cost operator. Low costs permit low prices, and low prices attract and retain good policyholders. The final segment of a virtuous circle is drawn when policyholders recommend us to their friends. GEICO gets more than one million referrals annually and these produce more than half of our new business" (Berkshire Hathaway 1996 letter). Note what is happening in those sentences: subjects act on objects; the abstract claim ("competitive strength") is immediately cashed out in mechanism ("low costs permit low prices") and in number ("one million referrals annually"). That is the voice. Imitate it.

**Principle 2: One specific testable example per abstract claim.** The discipline Munger describes in "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" — the talk he reworked in 2005 for *Poor Charlie's Almanack* — is to *invert*: don't reason from theory to fact; collect facts and then ask what theories survive them. Translate this into prose: every time you write an abstract sentence ("Costco has a wide moat"), the next sentence is the example that, if false, would falsify the claim ("Costco's 90% membership renewal rate has held within roughly two points across the 2008–2009 recession, the 2020 pandemic, and the 2022 inflation spike"). The reader should always know what would change your mind. Mauboussin's 2014 "What Does a Price-Earnings Multiple Mean? An Analytical Bridge between P/Es and Solid Economics" (Credit Suisse, January 29, 2014) makes this exact move with valuation: a multiple is not a number, it is a sentence about future cash flows, and you should be able to write that sentence.

**Principle 3: Industry is taught through the company, not bolted on as background.** Sell-side reports begin with a five-page macro chapter, then a five-page industry chapter, then introduce the company on page eleven. By then the reader has lost interest. Your writeup begins with the company and lets the industry emerge through the company's specific decisions: how Costco pays suppliers later than it collects from customers (negative working capital), why GEICO refuses to sell through agents (low-cost moat), how See's prices a pound of candy each year (untapped pricing power Munger loved). The reader learns boxed chocolates, auto insurance, and warehouse retail through the lens of one company in each — and ends up with sector intuition more durable than any McKinsey deck would have provided.

**Principle 4: Honesty about uncertainty without throat-clearing.** Throat-clearing is a tic: "while there are many factors to consider, and reasonable investors may disagree, in this report we will examine…" Cut all of it. Klarman's *Margin of Safety*, Chapter 8 ("The Art of Business Valuation"), gives the right tone: "Any attempt to value businesses with precision will yield values that are precisely inaccurate. The problem is that it is easy to confuse the capability to make precise forecasts with the ability to make accurate ones." Notice what Klarman does *not* do: he does not pad the sentence with "of course," "arguably," or "from our perspective." He just states the thing. That is the calibration. You are entitled to certainty when you have done the work, and entitled to humility when you have not, and you must be visibly precise about which is which.

**Principle 5: Numbers exist only in service of a claim.** A ratio with no story is decoration. Mauboussin's "Measuring the Moat" (originally December 2002, updated 2013 with Dan Callahan and re-released for Counterpoint Global / Morgan Stanley) is built on a single insight: a return on invested capital figure means nothing without a narrative about *why* it persists and *what* would erode it. ROIC of 30% at See's Candies is a story about California families buying Mother's Day boxes from 1972 to today; without that story, it is a spreadsheet output. Every number in your writeup should be the answer to a question — typically a per-customer, per-store, per-truck, or per-employee question — not a number you computed because the data was available.

A sixth, half-principle: **the writeup should pass the Cunningham test.** Lawrence Cunningham's editorial achievement in *The Essays of Warren Buffett* was to organize Buffett's letters thematically rather than chronologically — corporate governance, finance and investing, alternatives to common stocks, common stock, mergers and acquisitions, accounting, taxation. The implicit lesson is that great writing on a business sorts naturally into recurring categories of *owner concern*. If your writeup is organized around categories that would matter to a private owner — what we sell, who buys, who threatens us, who runs us, what we do with the cash — it sorts itself into Cunningham's structure. If it is organized around sell-side categories — earnings revisions, sentiment, technical setup — it does not.

## Part III — The structure: an opinionated section-by-section anatomy

The structure that follows is what excellent writeups in this tradition tend to converge on, even when they were not explicitly aiming at it. It is not a template; it is a sequence of *questions an owner would ask*, in roughly the order they would ask them. Skip a section only when you have a reason; do not add sections for the sake of length.

### 1. The lede: one paragraph, one mental model, one number

The first paragraph is the only paragraph some readers will internalize. Treat it that way. State, in roughly five sentences: what the company sells, who buys it, the unit of sale, the rough size of the customer base, and the one number that anchors the rest. The Microsoft-style template — "Microsoft sells Office to about 450 million office workers, who pay roughly $X per seat per month, mostly through their employer's IT budget rather than their own wallet" — is exactly right. It is concrete. It is testable. It produces an image.

A worked illustration in the See's voice: *See's Candies sells boxed chocolates — about 33 million pounds a year — almost entirely on the West Coast, mostly to people buying gifts in the four weeks before Christmas, Easter, Valentine's Day, and Mother's Day. Customers pay roughly $20 a pound at the register, in cash or by card, and walk out with the chocolate the same day; there is no shipping operation worth mentioning. The business needs about $40 million of capital to run, throws off most of its profits as cash, and has done so for fifty years (Berkshire 2007 letter).*

What the lede should *not* contain: a sector descriptor ("a leading consumer discretionary issuer"), a market-cap pleasantry ("a $X billion mid-cap"), or an analyst-flavored verb ("we initiate coverage with"). If a sentence could appear in any company's lede, it does not belong in this one.

### 2. Unit economics: how the business actually makes a dollar

After the lede, the question every owner asks is *show me the dollar*. This section answers it.

The right move is to choose the smallest reproducible economic unit — the per-store, per-truck, per-policyholder, per-software-seat — and walk it from revenue to cash. For Costco, the unit is the warehouse: roughly $250 million of annual sales, low single-digit operating margin, supplemented by the membership fee that drops largely to the bottom line, on a footprint that turns inventory faster than payables come due (the famous negative-working-capital flywheel Munger admired and that Charlie kept on Costco's board to watch over). For GEICO, the unit is the policy: the average premium, the expense ratio that runs roughly six points lower than the agent-driven competition, and the loss ratio that determines whether the year is profitable. For See's, the unit is a pound of candy: $1.85 in 1972, $5.42 by 1983, near $20 today, with volume creeping up at roughly 2% a year and price doing nearly all the lifting (Berkshire 1983 letter; 2007 letter).

Three rules govern this section.

First, *one number per paragraph*. Readers cannot retain three numbers in a paragraph; they will retain one number tied to a story. The discipline forces you to choose what matters.

Second, *make the number concrete by naming the unit*. "Operating margins of 8%" is forgettable. "On an average $130 basket, Costco keeps about $4 of operating profit and another $3 of membership fee — so the warehouse is really running on the membership and treating the merchandise as a near-loss-leader" is hard to forget.

Third, *connect each number to where the cash goes*. Buffett's owner earnings concept, introduced in the appendix to the 1986 letter ("Goodwill and its Amortization: The Rules and The Realities" extended in 1983), is the right discipline: reported earnings, plus non-cash charges, less the capital expenditure required to maintain the business at its current competitive position and unit volume. The point is not to compute the number to the dollar; it is to *track each dollar of profit to one of three places*: maintenance capex, growth reinvestment, or out the door to owners (dividends and buybacks). If your writeup leaves the reader unsure where the cash actually goes, you have not done this section yet.

A worked sentence: *FlightSafety, which Berkshire bought in 1996, earned $111 million pre-tax that year and held about $570 million of fixed assets; by 2007 pre-tax earnings had grown to $270 million but capital expenditures of $1.6 billion (mostly for $12-million-each flight simulators) had outpaced depreciation by hundreds of millions — the business grew, but every dollar of growth had to be paid for in advance with another simulator (Berkshire 2007 letter).* Notice the work that one sentence does: it tells the reader the business model (you sell training, but to sell training you must buy simulators), the capital intensity (fixed assets nearly six times pre-tax earnings), and the inescapable trade-off (growth requires reinvestment, so high reinvestment runway is good only if returns on that reinvestment exceed the cost).

### 3. The customer and demand section: Fisher's territory

This is the section that separates writeups by people who know the business from writeups by people who looked at the 10-K. Fisher's *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits* (Chapter 2, "What 'Scuttlebutt' Can Do") and his fifteen-point checklist (Chapter 3) are the founding documents for this section, and they are about a single move: go talk to people who don't work at the company.

Who buys the product? *Specifically* who. Not "consumers." A Costco customer is, on average, a forty-something-year-old household earning more than $100,000 a year, driving to a warehouse roughly twenty miles from home, spending about an hour per visit, leaving with rotisserie chicken and toilet paper and probably one impulse item like a kayak. A GEICO customer is, increasingly, a young adult who never wanted to talk to an insurance agent and clicks through an online quote in fifteen minutes. A FICO customer is not a consumer at all; it is a lender — a bank, a card issuer, a mortgage originator — embedding the FICO score in their underwriting workflow, paying pennies per pull but pulling billions of times.

Why do they stay? This is the moat question, dressed in customer clothing. Fisher's fifteen points (Chapter 3) spend most of their air on questions about whether the company is doing things that earn loyalty: are sales reps trained well, are profit margins worth defending, is research and development genuinely productive. The modern translation is: *what would a customer have to do to switch, and would they bother?* For Costco, switching means giving up about $1,500 per year of grocery savings to save $65 in membership fees; the math punishes leaving. For Microsoft Office, switching means retraining a forty-person finance team on a different spreadsheet; the cost is measured in weeks of productivity. For Verisign, "switching" off the .com top-level domain is not a customer choice at all; it is an internet-wide consensus that a domain you bought ten years ago for $8 a year still resolves to your website. Each of these is a different *kind* of stickiness, and naming the kind is the work of this section.

Notice what is happening here. By describing customers concretely, you have also begun to describe the moat — but from the demand side, where it is harder to fake. A moat sourced in actual customer behavior is more durable than a moat described from the company's marketing materials.

A worked sentence in the GEICO voice: *About one in three GEICO policyholders was referred by a friend or family member, which means GEICO acquires roughly half its new business at near-zero marginal cost — the math behind the gecko ads is that the ads are a tax on the business, not its engine, and the engine is the existing customer who is reasonably happy and tells someone else (Berkshire 1996 letter).*

### 4. The industry through the company's vantage

The mistake to avoid here is the pasted Porter five-forces (the framework, due to Michael Porter, that maps competitive intensity through five categories: rivalry, supplier power, buyer power, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes). It is intellectually inert when done as a checklist because it allows the writer to fill out five boxes without ever describing how the *specific* company faces these forces in its specific moment. Mauboussin and Callahan's "Measuring the Moat" actually uses Porter's vocabulary, but they always cash it out in industry-specific maps and profit-pool data — for example, showing that the U.S. airline industry has destroyed nearly $69 billion of economic profit in aggregate while a few specific links in the value chain (aircraft leasing, freight forwarding) have created profit. That is how to use the framework: as a question generator, never as a deliverable.

The opinionated move is to describe the industry *as the company sees it* — its profit pool, its threats, its few actually-relevant competitors — and to teach the reader the industry's economics through the company's choices. When Buffett describes the auto-insurance industry in the 1995 and 1996 letters, he does not list six competitors and their market shares. He says: it is a relatively expensive ($1,250 per policy in 1995), legally mandatory, undifferentiated product, in which the long-run winner will be whoever can deliver the same coverage at a lower cost — and then he describes how GEICO's no-agent model permanently structures that lower cost. By the end of the paragraph, the reader knows the auto-insurance industry better than they would after fifty pages of an industry primer.

A useful tactic here: identify the *one number that defines the industry's economics*. For airlines, it is load factor (and the related fact that empty seats spoil more completely than unsold widgets). For software, it is gross retention (because every churned dollar is a dollar of CAC permanently wasted). For boxed chocolates, it is per-capita consumption (which barely grows, which is *why* See's economic value comes from price, not volume — and why Buffett famously says "There aren't many See's in Corporate America," 2007 letter). Identify that number, anchor it to the company, and the industry suddenly has a shape.

### 5. Moat: Dorsey's four sources, named honestly

Pat Dorsey's *The Little Book That Builds Wealth* gives the cleanest taxonomy of durable competitive advantages: intangible assets (brand, patents, regulatory licenses), switching costs, network effects, and cost advantages. Dorsey's contribution was to make the categories *exhaustive* enough that you cannot wave at "moat" in the abstract — you have to say *which kind*, and then defend the choice.

The discipline this section demands is honesty about which of the four (if any) actually applies, and what would erode it.

Brand and intangibles: See's brand in California is real because customers prefer the candy "by an enormous margin" to alternatives, and "most lovers of chocolate prefer it to candy costing two or three times as much" (Berkshire 1983 letter). But the same brand is largely worthless east of the Rockies, where See's tried to expand and failed. So when you write that a company's moat is brand, ask: *brand among whom*? In what geography? At what price point? A brand moat that requires constant marketing to refresh is, as Buffett warned in the 2007 letter, "a moat that must be continuously rebuilt [and] eventually no moat at all." That sentence belongs taped to your monitor when you are writing about consumer companies.

Switching costs: the Microsoft Office moat is a switching-cost moat at the org chart level — it is the IT department, not the user, who decides whether you stay on Excel or move to Google Sheets, and the IT department has retraining costs and integration costs that price the user well above the user's own willingness to pay. State that clearly, and the reader understands why Office's per-seat price has held even as alternatives have technically improved.

Network effects: Verisign's hold on .com is not a network effect in the social-graph sense; it is a *coordination* effect — every browser, every email server, every existing domain registration assumes .com resolves through Verisign's infrastructure, and changing that assumption is a globally coordinated act that no one wants to undertake. Calling this a "network effect" without specifying *which network* and *what coordinates* is the kind of vagueness that lets writers feel insightful without saying anything.

Cost advantages: GEICO's moat is, fundamentally, a six-point distribution-cost advantage versus the agent-driven competition (Buffett quoted the figure variously across the 1976 internal memo, the 1995 letter, and the 1996 letter). That is *measurable* and *durable as long as the structural choice is made* — but it is not a permanent gift. The recent Fortune analysis (December 2025) noting that Progressive's data and pricing technology have eroded GEICO's loss-cost advantage — even as the distribution-cost advantage holds — is a useful cautionary tale. A cost-advantage moat is sturdy until competition finds a different cost line to attack.

This section's success criterion is simple: the reader should be able to name the moat type, point to the evidence, and state the thing that, if it changed, would mean the moat had eroded. Anything less is decoration.

### 6. Management and capital allocation: the Buffett owner-orientation tests

Buffett spends more space on management than almost any non-Buffett writer expects. Cunningham's organization of the *Essays* gives this its own large section, drawn from letters across decades. The reason is Buffett's belief, repeated almost annually, that capital allocation is most of what management actually does over time, and that competence at it is rare. The 1987 letter introduces Mr. Market — Graham's parable, retold to teach owners that the market quotes are weather, not wisdom — and immediately follows with a long discussion of capital allocation, because the only way to use Mr. Market is to know what your business is worth, which means knowing what management will do with each dollar of free cash flow.

Three questions, and only three, drive this section.

*Where has each retained dollar gone over the last ten years?* Track them. Reinvested into the existing business (capex, working capital), acquisitions, dividends, buybacks, debt paydown, or sitting in cash. For each bucket, what return has the company earned? This is brutally hard for diversified companies and brutally easy for focused ones — which is itself useful information. Mauboussin's repeated insistence that "incremental returns on capital should be prioritized over growth" ("The Math of Value and Growth," Counterpoint Global / Morgan Stanley) is the discipline here. A growing company that reinvests at 8% when it could buy back stock at an earnings yield of 12% is destroying value, however excited the press releases sound.

*Are the incentives aligned with owners?* This is where Munger's "incentive-caused bias" — Tendency #1 in his updated 2005 *Poor Charlie's Almanack* talk — does the work. Read the proxy statement carefully and *quote it*. If half of the CEO's compensation is tied to revenue growth and zero is tied to ROIC, expect revenue growth and price-insensitive acquisitions. If management owns shares it bought with its own money rather than shares it received in grants, the calculus is different. If insider sales over five years exceed insider buys by twenty to one, that is data, not noise. None of these are dispositive; all of them are information your future self will want to find easily.

*Has management told the truth in writing?* Read the last ten letters or transcripts. When the business missed, did management explain why concretely, or did they blame "macro headwinds"? Did they revisit their previous claims and grade them? Bloomstran's annual Semper Augustus letters (the 2024 letter ran more than 130 pages and devoted the customary substantial section to Berkshire) are themselves examples of this discipline — a writer who returns to old claims and either ratifies or amends them, in writing, where it can be checked. If management never grades themselves in public, they are unlikely to grade themselves in private.

A worked sentence: *Buffett bought Coca-Cola in 1988 at about $5 per share split-adjusted, and over the next thirty-five years, Coke distributed cash through dividends and buybacks at a pace that returned the entire purchase price several times over while the underlying business compounded its own intrinsic value at a slower but durable rate. The lesson is not that Coca-Cola was undervalued; it is that the management of those decades treated each retained dollar with a discipline that is, in his 1993 letter's word, "inevitable" only when the people running the business behave as if it is (Berkshire 1993 letter).*

### 7. Financial history as story, not table

The mistake here is the seven-year financial summary table. Tables compress information into rows the eye scans without reading. Replace the table with a narrative that walks the reader through the same data with attention to inflection points.

The four threads to weave:

- *Cash conversion*. Of every dollar of accounting profit, how much arrived as cash? When was the gap largest, and why? If a company's GAAP earnings have run consistently above its free cash flow for five years, ask what working-capital or capitalized-cost dynamic is driving the gap.
- *Return on incremental invested capital*. This is John Huber's recurring obsession at Saber Capital ("Calculating the Return on Incremental Capital Investments"). Total earnings growth divided by total reinvested capital across a period is a crude but sharp estimate of how productive the next dollar of retained earnings is likely to be. A company earning 30% on existing capital but only 10% on incremental capital is a company in slow decay; a company earning 15% on both is a company you can sleep through.
- *Reinvestment runway*. How many more dollars can this business productively absorb at returns above its cost of capital? See's, by Buffett's own admission, was a tiny runway — between 1972 and 2007 the business absorbed only $32 million of incremental capital despite generating $1.35 billion of pre-tax cash, because the boxed-chocolate market in California simply could not soak up more (Berkshire 2007 letter). That is a feature when paired with a parent that can deploy the cash elsewhere; it is a flaw if the cash gets reinvested in the wrong adjacencies.
- *Balance-sheet posture across cycles*. Did the company have to sell equity at the bottom in 2008–2009? Did it raise rescue financing in March–April 2020? Did it suspend its buyback during the inflation spike of 2022? A company's balance sheet behavior across cycles tells you how its management thinks about risk in a way no policy statement ever will. Marks's "Risk" memo (January 2006) and *The Most Important Thing* (especially Chapters 5–7 on risk understanding, recognition, and control) are the most useful primers on what to look for.

A worked sentence: *Markel, run for decades by the Markel family and then by Tom Gayner, has compounded book value per share at roughly 16% annually over twenty years by writing specialty insurance at an underwriting profit in fourteen of those twenty years and investing the float in publicly traded equities at attractive prices — a Berkshire-shaped business at a smaller scale, in which both engines must work for the model to work and both have, mostly, worked (John Huber, Saber Capital, "Markel (MKL) is a Compounding Machine").*

### 8. Risk: the pre-mortem, written in concrete English

Munger's contribution to investing thought that most distinguishes this section is his insistence on *inversion*. As he put it in the 1995 talk and again in the *Almanack* version of "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment": "Invert, always invert. … I sought good judgment mostly by collecting instances of bad judgment, then pondering ways to avoid such outcomes." Translate this into prose: write the section that imagines the business has died in five years, and tell the story of how. Then ask whether the story is plausible.

Three rules.

First, *every risk must be specific enough to be testable*. "Regulatory risk" is not a risk; it is a category. "The CFPB rescinds the 2017 prepaid-card rule, forcing the company to re-document 30% of its account base within twelve months" is a risk. The first you can ignore; the second has a probability and a magnitude.

Second, *separate the risks that would impair the business from the risks that would impair the price*. They are not the same. Buffett and Munger insist on this distinction because it is the entire content of Mr. Market: a stock can fall 40% in a year without the business value changing, and a stock can rise 40% while the business is quietly rotting. The 1987 letter's parable of Mr. Market and Marks's "On the Couch" (January 14, 2016) and "What Does the Market Know?" (January 19, 2016) are the canonical references. Your risk section should make clear which kind of risk each item is.

Third, *quantify the things you can quantify, and refuse to quantify the things you cannot*. Klarman's discipline in *Margin of Safety* Chapter 8 is the lodestar: any attempt to attach a probability to "the risk that AI commoditizes the company's product" will be precisely inaccurate. State the risk, describe the mechanism, name the leading indicators that would tell you it is happening, and stop. False precision is a form of dishonesty.

A worked paragraph in the See's voice: *The fastest way to break See's is gradual, not dramatic. The chocolate is sold mostly at four holidays a year, and most of the demand is gift-driven rather than self-purchased; if the cultural habit of giving boxed chocolates at Easter and Mother's Day fades over a generation — already visible among customers under thirty-five — volume erodes by a percent or two per year and pricing power, See's main lever, eventually meets a wall. There is no headline event to hedge; there is only the slow demographic question of whether the next generation gives the same gifts. Watch per-capita consumption in California, not stock price.*

### 9. Valuation: as expectations, never as a target

This is the section most often ruined. The standard sell-side error is to compute a target price by multiplying next-year EPS by an assumed P/E (price-to-earnings ratio), and pretend the result is a number. The standard academic error is to discount cash flows at a CAPM-derived cost of equity, with beta drawn from five years of historical price volatility, and pretend that result is a number. Both errors are in Klarman's gunsights in *Margin of Safety* Chapter 8, and both are the targets of Marks's repeated insistence that "volatility is the academic's choice for defining and measuring risk … because volatility is quantifiable and thus usable in the calculations and models of modern finance theory" — and that this convenience is the entire reason it is wrong ("Risk Revisited," 2014, building on the January 2006 "Risk" memo).

The right move is Mauboussin and Rappaport's, in *Expectations Investing* (revised 2021): start from the price, work backward to what the price implies must be true, and judge whether the implied future is more or less likely than your independent assessment of the future. As Mauboussin put it in the 2014 piece "What Does a Price-Earnings Multiple Mean?" — "multiples are not valuations but are a shorthand for the valuation process. The value of a financial asset is the present value of future cash flows." A 30x multiple is a sentence about what must happen for the next decade to justify it. Write that sentence. Then ask whether you believe it.

In practice, the section has three short parts.

First, *what does the price imply?* Translate the current price into expected sales growth, expected operating margin, expected investment rate, and the period over which excess returns persist (Mauboussin's "competitive advantage period" or, equivalently, the "T" in his framework). The numbers will be approximate; that is fine. A reverse DCF (discounted cash flow) is a useful exercise in producing the *implied* future, not the *forecast* future.

Second, *how does that compare to base rates and to your inside view of the company?* Mauboussin's *Base Rate Book* (Credit Suisse, 2016, with updated versions for Counterpoint Global) provides the outside-view discipline: most companies do not sustain 20% sales growth for ten years, because most companies in the historical sample did not. That base rate matters even when, especially when, the inside view says "this one is different." The right blend, per Kahneman and Lovallo via Mauboussin, is most of the weight on the outside view in luck-heavy domains, more weight on the inside view in skill-heavy ones.

Third, *what is the margin of safety?* Graham's central concept, restated by Klarman, is that you do not need to be precise; you need to be sufficiently wrong-able. If your independent assessment of the implied future is materially better than what the price requires, and you can describe the gap in plain English, you have a margin of safety. If you cannot describe the gap in plain English, you do not.

What this section does *not* contain: a target price; a sensitivity table; a CAPM cost of equity; a beta; a comp table of peers ranked by EV/EBITDA; the words "we recommend." None of those are useful to an owner in five years.

A worked sentence: *At today's price, the market is implying that Costco will compound revenue at roughly 8% for the next ten years, sustain its current operating margin, and continue earning above its cost of capital throughout — implications that have all proven true for the last twenty years and that nothing in the current business is visibly contradicting, but which leave little room for error if any of the three slips even modestly. The expected return is therefore close to the market's, and the margin of safety is in the durability of the business rather than in a price gap.*

### 10. The disconfirmation register: what would change my mind

This is the section sell-side reports never write and individual-investor writeups must. It is a numbered list of three to seven things — concrete, observable, falsifiable — that, if any of them happened, would force you to rethink the position. Membership renewal at Costco below 87%. GEICO's expense ratio rising more than three points above Progressive's. See's volume declining more than 3% per year for three consecutive years. Microsoft's commercial cloud growth dropping below 15%. Each item is a tripwire that you have agreed in advance to take seriously.

The discipline matters because of Munger's "Tendency 13: Deprival-Superreaction Tendency" and "Tendency 17: Inconsistency-Avoidance Tendency" (*Poor Charlie's Almanack*). Investors are systematically bad at changing their minds in real time; pre-committing on paper to what would change your mind is the cheapest and most powerful debiasing technique available. It is the single section in the writeup most likely to save you money in the next five years.

The list should be short — Pabrai's *The Dhandho Investor* makes the point that few bets, big bets, infrequent bets is the right posture for ideas, and the same logic applies to disconfirmers: too many tripwires and none of them get respected.

## Part IV — Numbers as story: the discipline that holds the rest together

The single most common failure mode in well-meaning writeups is the inability to put a number down without immediately surrounding it with three more. That habit is endemic in sell-side culture — every claim is hedged with three comparisons, every margin is footnoted with a peer set — and it actively destroys the writeup's usefulness to its real reader.

Three governing rules.

**Anchor every number to a unit of human-scale reality.** Per-customer, per-store, per-truck, per-employee, per-policy, per-domain. The reason See's is teachable is that Buffett tells you the price *per pound* and the volume *in pounds*, and a pound is something a human being holds in a hand. The reason Costco is teachable is that the warehouse and the membership are visible, countable units. Translate every aggregate financial line into the smallest reproducible unit before reporting it.

**One number per paragraph; ratios in service of a claim.** A paragraph that says "operating margin is 15.2%, gross margin is 47.1%, EBITDA margin is 22.3%, free cash flow margin is 18.7%" has communicated nothing. A paragraph that says "of every $100 of revenue, the company keeps about $15 of operating profit and converts roughly that whole $15 to cash" has communicated the entire economic shape. Use ratios when they answer a question the previous sentence has raised; otherwise drop them.

**Refuse to compute when the inputs do not warrant it.** Klarman's "precisely inaccurate" warning is the clearest version. If you cannot defend the discount rate, do not produce a DCF. If you cannot defend the multiple, do not produce a target price. The most professional move in the whole tradition is the willingness to write "we don't know" in places where lesser writers fill in numbers (Marks's recurring "I don't know" school, articulated most explicitly in "I Beg to Differ," July 2022, and across multiple memos cataloged in his "The Best of …" 2021 retrospective).

The animating concept here is Mauboussin and Rappaport's expectations investing: the market price is the unknown that you are trying to interrogate, not a number you are trying to outguess. Buffett puts the same point in homelier language in the 1987 letter, when he reminds you that Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to instruct you. Numbers in a writeup are most useful when they are the *answer* to the question "what does the price imply must be true, and is that more or less likely than the future I see?"

A short illustration of the rules in compact form: *Domino's, in the version Mauboussin and Rappaport use as a case study in the revised Expectations Investing, was at one point priced for a fifteen-year competitive advantage period at high single-digit revenue growth. An owner could either accept that and ask whether the franchise model could deliver, or back out and price the company at a five-year window — the latter producing a fair value roughly half the former. The exercise is not to compute the right answer; it is to make the assumption that produces the price visible enough to argue with.*

## Part V — Industry as apprenticeship: how to teach the sector through one company

Probably the most underrated benefit of doing this kind of writing is that it apprentices you in the industry. After one careful 8,000-word writeup of GEICO, you understand auto insurance better than most journalists who cover the industry full-time. After one writeup of Costco, you understand warehouse retail and the negative-working-capital model in a way that makes you immediately suspicious of, say, restaurant chains that do *not* have that structure but pretend the unit economics are similar. After one writeup of FICO, you know more about the structure of consumer credit than a reasonably informed banker.

This works for a specific reason: a company is a *bound* problem and an industry is *unbound*. You cannot read the boxed-chocolate industry; there is no 10-K for it. But you can read See's deeply enough that the industry shows itself through See's choices — the four-holiday concentration, the West Coast geography, the brand premium that does not travel east, the volume stagnation paired with pricing power. Every company-level fact is a sector-level fact in disguise. If you write the company patiently, the sector becomes legible.

Some practical moves to make this happen on the page.

**Use the company's competitors as the sector's variation.** Don't cover them in their own section; introduce each one at the moment you need them to clarify a point about the company. Progressive enters the GEICO writeup at the moment you describe loss costs, because that is where the comparison teaches. Walmart enters the Costco writeup at the moment you describe SKU count and inventory velocity, because the contrast is the lesson. By the end, the reader has met four competitors, each in the place where they illuminate something — and has therefore mapped the sector.

**Use historical analogs, openly named.** Buffett does this constantly: See's reminds him of Coca-Cola; the Buffalo News reminds him of small-town newspapers in the 1970s; Apple reminds him of consumer brands he has known. Marks does it with cycles: 2007 reminds him of 1998, 1973, 1929. The analog is the device that makes a single business teach the genus to which it belongs. Use them. *"The Costco model resembles the See's model in one specific respect: in both, price discipline rather than volume growth is the engine, and management's job is not to grow but to refuse to extract."*

**Resist the temptation to be comprehensive.** A writeup that tries to teach the entire industry is a worse industry teacher than a writeup that teaches one company well. Pat Dorsey's *The Little Book That Builds Wealth* is teachable precisely because each of his four moat sources is illustrated through a small number of carefully chosen companies, not through a survey of the S&P 1500. Apprenticeship is depth, not breadth.

A consequence of this principle worth naming: **honest writing about your circle of competence as it expands**. Buffett's 1996 sentence about boundaries applies most acutely to the writer who is *learning*. If you are writing about your first auto-insurance company, your writeup should be visibly more cautious than your fifth — and the way it shows that caution is by limiting the range of things you claim to understand. This is the antidote to imposter syndrome: not bravado, but accurate boundaries. *"This is the first auto-insurance company I have studied in detail. The unit economics described here are derived from the company's own disclosures and I have not yet stress-tested them against a long enough peer sample to be confident in the comparisons. I will revisit this in 2027 when I have read at least three more such companies."* That is a permitted sentence. It earns trust because it accurately describes what the writer knows.

## Part VI — Voice and prose: the small earned surprise

The voice question is not aesthetic; it is functional. Sentences that read well are sentences that the reader will retain, and retention is the whole game.

Five concrete techniques.

**Concrete subjects act on concrete objects.** "Microsoft sells Office to about 450 million office workers." "GEICO writes auto insurance directly to consumers without paying commissions to agents." "Costco extracts a $65 annual fee from sixty-something million households." Each sentence has a person or company doing a specific thing to a specific object. This is the Buffett voice — read his 1996 letter on GEICO again — and it is the voice you should imitate. The opposite, what to avoid: passive constructions ("the product is sold"), hedged abstractions ("the company offers a range of solutions"), and noun-pile jargon ("a vertically integrated, omnichannel value-chain participant").

**One example per abstract claim, named in the next sentence.** Every time you make a generalization, the next sentence anchors it. "Customer concentration creates risk." → "The company's top three customers account for 41% of revenue, and the largest, a single national retailer, accounted for 19% in 2024." The pattern is so simple it can become a discipline: write the abstract, type a colon, write the example. Where the example does not exist or you cannot remember it, the abstract was probably wrong.

**Define jargon inline, on first use, in plain English.** The reader is your future self, who has forgotten what NDR (net dollar retention — the percentage of last year's recurring revenue you still have this year, after churn but with expansion within existing customers) means. So define it. Inline. The rule extends to acronyms (CAC = customer acquisition cost; LTV = lifetime value; DSO = days sales outstanding; ARR = annual recurring revenue). The reader will thank you in 2031, and so will their spouse at dinner.

**Sentence rhythm matters.** Strings of fifteen-word sentences read like accounting prose. Strings of forty-word sentences read like academic prose. The Buffett-Marks voice mixes them: a short declarative sentence delivers the claim, a longer sentence elaborates with mechanism, a short sentence lands the lesson. Read three Buffett letters aloud and you will hear it. Read the Marks "On the Couch" memo aloud and you will hear it more cleanly because his voice is more consistently rhythmic. Imitate.

**The small earned surprise.** This is the most important technique and the hardest to teach. A great writeup contains, somewhere in its middle, a single observation that the reader did not expect and could not have predicted — but which, on reflection, is obviously true and powerfully clarifying. Buffett's surprises are often inversions: See's is "a more mundane business … with $2 million of earnings on $18 million of net tangible assets" reflected back at the same earnings on $8 million of net tangible assets — and "the answer is 'yes' even if both businesses were expected to have flat unit volume" (1983 letter). Marks's surprises are usually about consensus: "every once in a while, the market needs a trip to the shrink" ("On the Couch," January 2016). Mauboussin's surprises are usually about base rates: most growth companies do not sustain growth, even when their product is great. The surprise is "earned" because it is supported by the prior 2,000 words; if it landed in the lede, it would not have been a surprise. The surprise is the moment the reader trusts the writer for the rest of their life.

You produce a small earned surprise by writing the boring 80% first and then asking, near the end of the draft: *what is the thing I have just learned that the average reader, even an informed one, would find counter to expectation?* Then move that thing to where it lands hardest — typically the end of the moat section or the beginning of the risk section. One per writeup. Two would be greedy.

**On uncertainty without throat-clearing.** State the uncertainty. Move on. "I do not know whether the next CEO will allocate capital with the same discipline as the current one. The current one has allocated capital with above-average discipline since 2014, by the test of incremental ROIC; that is the evidence I have. The risk that this changes is one of the three things I would track most closely after a CEO transition." That paragraph is honest, useful, and lacks every form of hedging language except the actual hedge. Read it next to the standard sell-side equivalent — "while there can be no assurance that capital allocation discipline will be maintained, our base case assumes continuity" — and the difference is the difference between writing for a reader and writing to avoid a lawsuit.

## Part VII — What to leave out

This may be the most important section, because the failure mode of well-meaning writeups is not omission; it is over-inclusion. The discipline is to delete.

**Delete the target price.** A target price is a fiction the writer pretends is a fact. Buffett does not have target prices for Coca-Cola; he has expectations. Marks does not have target prices for Oaktree's distressed debt portfolios; he has scenarios. Klarman's whole *Margin of Safety* Chapter 8 is an argument against the target price as a category. The reasons are these: target prices anchor your psychology, so when the market price reaches the target you sell rather than reassessing; target prices give an illusion of analytical precision the underlying analysis does not actually possess; and, most damaging, target prices encourage the writer to treat the writeup as a forecast of price rather than a description of business value. The right substitute is the expectations-investing frame: state what the price implies, state what you believe, state the gap.

**Delete CAPM-derived discount rates.** Beta — the historical price volatility relative to the market that academic finance treats as the measure of risk — is not what risk *is* for a long-term holder. Marks's "Risk" (January 2006) and "Risk Revisited" (2014) make the case sharply: "if you define risk as anything other than volatility, it can't be measured even after the fact." That is not a complaint; it is the point. Risk is the probability of permanent capital loss, and you assess it by understanding what could permanently impair the business. A discount rate that comes out of the CAPM is a number derived from a model that defines risk in a way that contradicts how Buffett, Marks, Klarman, and Munger actually think about risk. Do not import it into your writing. If you must use a discount rate, use the long-term return you require to take this risk — typically 8–12% for equity-like exposures — and *say* that is what you are doing.

**Delete sensitivity tables.** A sensitivity table — the standard sell-side grid showing valuation outputs across rows of growth rates and columns of multiples — is impressive only to readers who do not understand that the row inputs and the column inputs are not actually independent. A higher growth rate typically implies a higher sustainable multiple; a higher discount rate typically reflects circumstances that would also lower growth. The grid implies that any cell in it could be the answer; reality only allows a ribbon of cells along a diagonal. Worse, the table tells the reader nothing about *which* cell to weight more than another. Replace it with a single paragraph naming three scenarios — base, optimistic, pessimistic — each with one or two sentences of mechanism. The reader can hold three scenarios; they cannot hold thirty.

**Delete SWOT and the pasted Porter's five forces.** The SWOT (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) framework was not designed for this purpose; it was designed for managers planning a department's quarterly retreat. As a writeup section it produces inventories — three bullet points under each heading — that masquerade as analysis. The five forces are more rigorous in origin but become inert in the same way when pasted in as a section heading. Mauboussin and Callahan's "Measuring the Moat" uses Porter's vocabulary but never as an inert template; they use it to *generate* questions about industry structure that they then answer with company-specific evidence. That is the right relationship to any framework. If you cannot put the framework's vocabulary to work in the company's specific situation, leave the vocabulary out.

**Delete generic ESG framing.** ESG (environmental, social, and governance) topics matter to the business when they matter to the business — when a regulatory shift changes unit economics, when a labor-relations failure affects retention, when a governance failure produces capital misallocation. They do not matter as abstract ratings imported from a third-party scoring service. The right move is to address each item exactly where it lives: governance in the management section, environmental exposure in the risk section, labor in the unit economics or customer section if it bears on those. Cordoning ESG off as its own section signals that you are checking a box rather than doing the analysis. The Buffett-Munger tradition is comfortable taking governance extremely seriously — it is most of what Cunningham's *Essays* organizes — and treating "ESG" as an abstraction extremely lightly. Match that calibration.

**Delete throat-clearing prose.** "In this report, we will examine …" "It is important to note that …" "The purpose of this section is to explore …" "From the perspective of a long-term investor …" Every one of these phrases is the writer warming up. Cut them. Begin each section with the first thing you actually have to say. If you have nothing to say, the section does not need to exist.

**Delete the 'recommendation.'** You are not recommending anything. You are describing a business. The reader can decide what to do with the description. The act of slapping a "Buy / Hold / Sell" rating on the writeup turns a description into a directive and forecloses the future thinking the writeup is supposed to enable. The position you take is implicit in the work; it does not need to be stamped at the top.

The collective effect of these deletions is striking. A typical sell-side report runs 25–60 pages; an excellent individual-investor writeup of similar analytical depth runs 15–35 pages, because most of the sell-side report was throat-clearing, framework-pasting, and ratings ceremony. The space saved is space available for the actual business.

## Part VIII — Worked micro-examples in the masters' voice

The clearest way to demonstrate the voice is to write short paragraphs in it about businesses the masters have written about. Each of the following is one paragraph; each demonstrates the rules of one section.

*Lede, See's:* "See's Candies sells about 33 million pounds of boxed chocolates a year, almost all of it on the West Coast, almost all of it as a gift, almost all of it during the four weeks before Christmas, Easter, Valentine's Day, and Mother's Day. Customers pay roughly $20 a pound at one of about 200 retail shops, the supply chain is two-step (kitchen-to-shop), and the entire operation runs on about $40 million of capital — a number that has barely moved since 1972, even as pre-tax profits have risen from $5 million to north of $80 million. The business is the closest thing in Berkshire Hathaway's portfolio to an ATM that prints in proportion to U.S. inflation."

*Unit economics, Costco:* "A typical Costco warehouse does about $250 million of merchandise sales a year, on which it earns roughly $7 million of operating profit — a margin so thin that the warehouse, considered alone, is barely profitable. The real business is membership: about $65 a year from each of 70 million households, almost all of which renews. The warehouse is a giant lure for membership renewal; the membership is the actual product; and because the warehouse collects from the customer immediately and pays its suppliers thirty days later, growth requires no working capital — every new warehouse pays for its own inventory."

*Customer/demand, GEICO (1996 voice):* "GEICO acquires customers without paying commissions to agents, so its cost structure is roughly six percentage points lighter than the agent-driven industry average. Lower cost permits lower price; lower price attracts customers; happy customers refer their friends; the company gets more than a million referrals a year, which is more than half of new business. The flywheel is decades old, and Tony Nicely (CEO at the time of writing) has organized the entire enterprise around protecting the cost line — not the marketing budget — because the cost line is the moat (Berkshire 1996 letter)."

*Industry through company, Coca-Cola:* "When the Coca-Cola Company went public in 1919 at $40 a share, it sold about 116,000 gallons of syrup. By the end of 1920 the stock had been cut in half. By 1938 the same share had grown into $3,277 of value with dividends reinvested; by 1993, $25,000. The company's century is a single demonstration that the soft-drink industry, properly executed, is structured around brand and distribution rather than product novelty — and that 'inevitable' growth requires only the absence of management mistakes large enough to break a global supply chain. The lesson is not that Coca-Cola was undervalued in 1988; it is that Coca-Cola was inevitable, and Buffett paid 18× earnings for the inevitability (Berkshire 1993 letter)."

*Moat, Microsoft Office:* "Microsoft Office's moat is not the software; it is the org chart. The decision-maker who chooses Office is the IT director, not the user; the IT director's switching cost is measured in months of finance-team retraining and integration debugging; and Microsoft prices to a level that is high enough to harvest the switching cost but low enough that no IT director wakes up angry about it. The result is a per-seat price that has held within a narrow band for fifteen years even as the technical alternatives have improved. The moat will erode when, and only when, the locus of decision-making moves from the IT director to the user — which AI-native productivity tools may eventually accomplish, but have not yet."

*Management, Berkshire 2007-style on capital allocation:* "Charlie and I are fond of saying that the capital allocation skill of any given CEO matters more than every other operating skill they possess, because over a decade in the chair the CEO will allocate something like the company's market capitalization in retained earnings, and the cumulative quality of that allocation drives terminal book value more than any individual operational decision. We look at how the CEO has used cash over the past five years — not in their press releases, in the cash flow statement — and we read the letters to see whether they have graded their own allocation decisions or only celebrated them."

*Risk, See's pre-mortem:* "The way See's dies is generational. Boxed chocolates are a holiday-gift category, and the cohort buying them today skews older than the cohort that bought them in 1990, which skewed older than the cohort that bought them in 1972. If a generation arrives that does not give boxed chocolates as a default Mother's Day gift, See's volume erodes by a percent or two a year for twenty years and pricing power runs out of its slack — but no headline announces the change, and the company will report perfectly fine quarters until the day it doesn't. The leading indicator is per-capita consumption among under-thirty-fives in California; the lagging indicator is the only one anyone reports."

*Valuation as expectations, Costco:* "At today's price, Costco trades at a multiple that implies the next decade looks substantially like the last one — about 8% revenue growth, stable operating margin, continued membership renewal above 90%. Each of those is more likely than not, individually; collectively they leave little headroom. The expected return at this price is therefore close to the long-run market return, with a margin of safety derived not from a price gap but from the durability of the underlying business. If the price falls by 25% on a market panic that does not impair any of the three drivers, the position becomes materially more attractive; that is the kind of opportunity to wait for, not the kind of opportunity to manufacture."

*Disconfirmation register, GEICO:* "I would rethink the GEICO position if any one of the following occurred: (1) GEICO's expense ratio rose more than three points above Progressive's for two consecutive years; (2) the loss-cost gap closed entirely, meaning Progressive's combined ratio became permanently better than GEICO's by more than two points; or (3) the percentage of new policies acquired through referrals fell below 30%. Each of these is observable in the company's own disclosures within four quarters of happening, and each would indicate that the underlying mechanics of the moat had shifted — not that the stock had moved."

These are stylistic targets. Your own paragraphs do not need to imitate the cadence; they need to match the *posture* — concrete subjects, concrete objects, one specific testable example per abstract claim, a number tied to a unit, jargon defined inline, no throat-clearing, and the reader treated as someone who is on your side and short of time.

## Part IX — The checklist question: the writeup is the answer

Michael Shearn's *The Investment Checklist* and Mohnish Pabrai's checklist tradition (*The Dhandho Investor*, drawing partly on Atul Gawande's *Checklist Manifesto* and partly on Munger) have produced an enormous amount of useful prompt material — Shearn's book contains roughly 70 questions across nine chapters; Pabrai's lecture-circuit checklist runs to nearly a hundred items. Both authors are explicit that the point is not the checklist itself but the *attention* it forces.

The right relationship to checklists for this style of writeup is therefore subordinate: the checklist is scaffolding the reader does not see. You use it during research to make sure you have not skipped a question; you do not reproduce it in the document. A writeup that consists of seventy questions and seventy answers reads like a tax form; a writeup that *implicitly* answers every question while reading like a story is what you want.

Three practical rules.

**Use the checklist before drafting, not after.** Before you start writing, run through Shearn's nine areas and Pabrai's question list and ask which ones you cannot answer yet. The unanswered ones are your remaining research, not your remaining drafting.

**Write the document as continuous prose, then check off the questions in your head.** The questions become the *test* of the document, not its scaffolding. If, after drafting, you cannot find an answer to "do customers buy this product willingly, or under duress?", the document is not done — but the answer needs to live inside a sentence in the customer section, not in a Q&A box.

**Make the disconfirmation register the most checklist-like part.** The "what would change my mind" section is the only place the writeup should resemble a list, because the items there work better as discrete tripwires than as woven prose. Keep that section short and cut everything else into paragraphs.

The result is a document where everything Shearn and Pabrai would want you to think about is *present*, but nothing they would want you to ask is *visible* as a question. The reader gets the analytical rigor without the form-filling experience.

## Part X — Revising the writeup: it is a living document

The writeup is dated and signed at the top, and it is revised. Pretending otherwise is the fastest way to make it useless.

Three revision practices.

**Date every significant claim, and revisit it on a schedule.** Bloomstran's annual Semper Augustus letters are exemplary: he revisits earlier predictions explicitly, names which were right and which were wrong, and updates his current views in the same document. The 2014 letter that revisited his 2000 predictions ("Twelve Predictions from 2000 Revisited"), in particular, is a model of writing-against-yourself. Imitate this in miniature: when you update a writeup, do not silently overwrite the prior view; mark what you previously thought, what you now think, and what changed your mind. The audit trail is the entire point.

**Distinguish three kinds of revision.** Editorial (typos, clarity); factual (a number changed, a CEO retired); and analytical (you changed your mind about something material). Each requires different treatment. Editorial fixes can be silent. Factual updates should be marked with a date stamp. Analytical revisions should include a short paragraph naming the prior view, the new view, and the reason for the change. *"In the 2024 version of this writeup I argued that the company's loss-cost advantage was structural and durable. Two years of post-pandemic data have made me less confident: the gap has closed by roughly two points, and the closure appears to reflect Progressive's investment in usage-based pricing, which GEICO has been slow to match. I now describe the cost advantage as one-pillar (distribution) rather than two-pillar (distribution plus loss-cost), and the disconfirmation register has been updated accordingly."* That kind of paragraph is what makes the document earn its place on your hard drive.

**Do not turn the document into an apology when you have been wrong.** Munger's "Tendency 17: Inconsistency-Avoidance" pushes us toward an opposite failure mode: when we have been wrong, we are tempted to over-correct, tear down the prior writeup, and treat the new view as definitive. Resist. Marks's "It's All a Big Mistake" (June 20, 2012) makes the case that *every* investment transaction implies a mistake on someone's part — yours, the counterparty's, both — and that the question is which mistakes are recoverable and which are not. Treat each revision as an iteration, not a confession. The right mood for revising is cool, factual, and forward-looking. *"This was wrong, here is what I learned, here is what I will watch next time."* No more, no less.

The single best discipline I know for keeping the writeup honest over time is to set a calendar reminder for one year after publication that says: *re-read your own writeup. Mark anything that has aged badly. Don't fix it yet — just mark it.* Then, one week later, sit down and revise. The week of distance produces an editor where there used to be only an author.

## Part XI — How to know when a writeup is finished

The honest answer is: never, completely. But a writeup is *useful* — which is the lower bar that matters — when it passes a single test.

**The five-year test.** Imagine you have not thought about this company in three or four years. A piece of news arrives — an earnings miss, a CEO change, a regulatory action, a takeover bid. You re-read your writeup, in one sitting, and ask: *do I know what to do?*

If the writeup tells you what to do, it works. If it does not, something is missing. The most common things missing are: the disconfirmation register (so you do not know which kind of news matters); the unit economics in human-scale form (so you do not know whether the news affects the dollar); the management section grounded in evidence (so you cannot judge whether to trust the new CEO's stated plan); or, most often, the moat section's specificity (so you cannot tell whether the news is the kind of news that erodes the moat or the kind that is noise).

A second test, complementary: **the dinner test.** After re-reading the writeup, you should be able to explain to your spouse, in roughly five minutes, what the company sells, who buys it, why customers stay, where the cash goes, and what would break it. If the explanation requires you to consult the document, the document is not yet doing its job.

A third test, the most demanding: **the Cunningham test.** If the *Essays of Warren Buffett* model is to organize a body of writing thematically rather than chronologically — corporate governance here, finance and investing there, accounting in another section — then your writeup, considered alongside the other writeups you produce over a decade, should sort cleanly into the same thematic shelves. The discipline of writing well about one company makes you a better writer about all companies; the discipline of writing well about all companies makes you a better owner of the single position that, fifteen years from now, will turn out to be the one that mattered most.

## Coda: the spirit of the thing

What separates this tradition from the sell-side tradition, in the end, is a difference in *who the writing is for*.

The sell-side analyst writes for an audience of paying institutional clients who are, on average, slightly suspicious of the analyst, will not remember the report next month, and are most interested in whether the analyst's price target ranks above or below the consensus. The optimal writing strategy under those constraints is: hedge everything, defer to the model, anchor on the consensus, deliver a deliverable. The work product is the deliverable.

The individual long-term investor writes for an audience of one — themselves, in five years, holding a position they only half-remember the original case for. The optimal writing strategy under those constraints is: be specific, be honest, be useful, deliver a document the future reader can act on. The work product is the future decision.

Buffett is the master case because his audience is even smaller. He writes for himself, for Charlie (until 2023), and for shareholders who, by the time they have read the second annual letter, are already aligned partners. He has no need to hedge, no need to impress, no need to demonstrate sophistication; his only need is to be useful to his future self and to the partners reading. Read the 1983, 1986, 1987, 1988, 1989, 1993, 1996, and 2007 letters in order, and you are watching forty years of *the same posture* — the posture of someone writing to be useful — produce essays that are still teaching, decades later. Cunningham's *Essays of Warren Buffett* organizes them; the *Almanack* extends the same posture to Munger; *The Most Important Thing* pulls it through to Marks; *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits* anchors it back to Fisher; Mauboussin's research adds the analytical scaffolding; Klarman, Pabrai, Greenblatt, Dorsey, and Shearn give it operational and pedagogical extensions; the modern Substack generation — Mostafa Soliman at MBI Deep Dives, Drew Cohen at Speedwell Research, Chris Bloomstran at Semper Augustus, John Huber at Saber Capital, the writers at Investment Talk and Junto Investments and Sleep Well Investments and Value Stock Geek and Ensemble Capital — is reproducing it for a new generation in a new format, and proving that the posture transfers.

That posture is the answer to the original question. Excellent single-company research, in this tradition, looks like a document that an owner could hand to a fellow owner and trust it to be useful. It is concrete, concretely numbered, narratively organized, intellectually honest, opinionated where opinion is earned, humble where humility is earned, and visibly the work of someone who has thought about one specific business hard enough to teach it.

The good news is that the standard is reproducible. The bad news is that the only way to reproduce it is to write — to draft, to revise, to delete the throat-clearing, to hunt down the specific number, to find the historical analog that lands, to date the disconfirmation register, to revisit the document a year later and grade it. The work is real, and slow, and rewarded slowly.

If you have read this far, the practical instruction is simple: pick one company within your circle of competence, allocate a month to it, and write the writeup as if your future self in 2031 were reading it under conditions of mild stress. Then put it away. Then, in 2027, read it. The writeup that you return to two years later and recognize as still useful is the writeup you have learned to write. From there it compounds — like the businesses themselves, slowly and almost invisibly, until one day you look up and notice that you understand more than you used to, and the documents on your hard drive are an asset rather than a stack of paper.

That is the operating manual. Now go write.
